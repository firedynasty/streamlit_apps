# Structured Knowledge Document: Tech Layoffs, AI Impact, and Career Perspectives (2025)

## Key Facts & Decisions

- 126,000+ tech jobs eliminated in 2025 (per layoffs.fyi data)
- Layoffs were "precise, not random" - described as a "value audit"
- Companies asked "Who keeps the lights on?" not "Who works hard?"
- Multiple rounds of layoffs across big tech companies

- 25 years experience in tech industry
- Recently laid off from big tech (approximately December 2025, ~2 weeks before first video)
- Started coding in high school, worked through college
- Career progression: developer → tech lead → enterprise architect → senior technical leadership
- 7 years at big tech company before layoff
- Has patents to his name
- Earned six-figure salary most of career, "absolutely insane amounts of money" at big tech (RSUs)
- Mid-40s age, married, has children including a teenage son who's STEM-inclined
- Wife recently had health crisis, became partially disabled
- Location: San Francisco, California, US (based on context)

- Pursuing FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) strategy

- Was on layoff list along with junior colleagues

## Q&A Pairs

### Q: Which tech roles were hit hardest in 2025 layoffs?
A: 
- Frontend-only engineers with shallow system exposure
- Junior data analysts who mostly built dashboards
- QA roles dependent on manual testing
- "Prompt engineers" hired during AI gold rush
- Middle managers who owned process, not outcomes

### Q: Which tech roles survived or grew despite layoffs?
A:
- Infrastructure-focused backend engineers
- Security and compliance specialists
- Data engineers who ship pipelines, not slides
- Platform engineers who reduce cloud bills
- Engineers who can debug production under pressure

### Q: What are the 7 "untouchable" skills according to the Medium article?
A:
1. **Systems thinking** - Understanding how everything breaks, tracing requests browser → API → queue → database → cache, reading logs like a story
2. **Production ownership** - Being on-call, responding calmly, fixing issues, writing postmortems, preventing recurrence
3. **Data engineering over analysis** - Building ETL/ELT pipelines, streaming ingestion, data quality checks, cost-aware storage design
4. **Cloud cost optimization** - Right-sizing instances, killing zombie resources, optimizing queries, reducing data egress (one engineer saved $200k on AWS)
5. **Security literacy** - Understanding auth flow, token lifetime, least privilege, preventing "just expose it internally" mistakes
6. **AI as leverage** - Using AI to debug faster, generate test cases, explore edge cases - NOT branding yourself as "AI engineer"
7. **Clear thinking under pressure** - Asking right questions, ignoring noise, choosing least-worst options, communicating clearly

### Q: What are the engineer's three options post-layoff?
A:
**Option 1: Get another big tech job**
- Only upside: "bonkers levels of compensation"
- Applied extensively: mostly silence, automated rejections within 10 minutes
- Got ONE screener after weeks, passed it, scheduled technical assessment
- Interviewer: architect in early 30s
- Decision: **Rejected this option** - "all downsides," one upside not worth it
- Reason: The "Bob" realization (see Bob story below)

**Option 2: Early retirement (FIRE)**
- Can live comfortably without working due to 17 years savings
- Upsides: Time freedom, family, health, hobbies, relationships
- Challenges:
  - Black swan risk: AI bubble could cause depression, render assets worthless
  - AI success could create "technofuedalism" - everyone becomes "surfs to AI empowered masters"
  - Most men need meaning through building/creating
- Status: "Default option" since not currently working

**Option 3: Meaningful work**
- Take job with significant cause, or work on own projects/frameworks
- Downside: Need to shift mindset from "doer" to "founder/business owner"
- **DECISION: Pursuing combination of Option 2 + 3** - Semi-retire while working on meaningful side projects
- Approach: "Throw a bunch of stuff on the wall and see what sticks"

### Q: What is the "Bob" story and why was it pivotal?
A:
**The Story (2010):**
- Engineer (then early 30s, just promoted to architect) interviewed "Bob" (man in 50s)
- Bob had gray hair, gray eyes, "certain edginess"
- Engineer was "super hard interviewer," grilled Bob extensively
- Made Bob implement all major design patterns (factory, singleton, prototype)
- Tested on concurrency, multi-threading, LRU hashmap from scratch
- Bob handled everything "with flying colors," knew Java "like back of his hand"
- Bob knew all major frameworks (Struts, Spring, Guice - "hardly anyone knew Guice")
- **Revealed**: Bob worked on original Java language and JVM at Sun Microsystems in early 90s
- Bob had removed this experience from resume "to appear more relevant and find a job"
- Bob hadn't worked for over a year
- Team agreed Bob was strong, but mentioned "edginess," seemed "pushy or aggressive"
- Hiring manager conclusion: "Really strong candidate, but may not be a good cultural fit"
- Company was mostly people in 20s and early 30s

**The Realization:**
- "Bob wasn't being pushy or aggressive. Bob was desperate."
- "Aging engineer trying really hard to prove his worth and get a job"
- "Things have come full circle and I've turned into Bob"
- "Bob, if you're still out there, I respect you, man. I don't want to be you, but I respect you."
- This realization made him unable to continue pursuing big tech jobs

### Q: What is the engineer's assessment of current AI capabilities?
A:
**What AI Is:**
- Probabilistic, not logical
- Predicts most likely next word based on training data
- "No logic or reasoning happening at all"
- "Idiot savant capable of parroting logic and reason"
- "No understanding" in the model

**What AI Is Not:**
- Not on linear path to AGI (Artificial General Intelligence)
- Not a "machine god"
- Scientists haven't figured out how human brain does reasoning/logic
- Theory of LLMs → AGI via scaling is "really far-fetched"

**Key Insight:**
- "Don't need AGI to totally disrupt tech industry"
- Analogy: 19th century agriculture mechanization didn't need humanoid robots, just tractors
- "Vast majority of software is repetitive and unimaginative, kind of like what farmers did"
- Software is digital = "incredibly easy to use as training data"
- "Software industry should be one of the easiest for current AI to replace"

### Q: What are AI's current limitations in software development?
A:
1. **Language popularity gaps:**
   - Popular languages (JavaScript, Python, Java) have more training data, perform better
   - Rare languages (COBOL, Apex) have more hallucinations, performance issues

2. **Training data quality:**
   - Bell curve: small amount of modern code, way more old/deprecated code
   - Lots of inconsistent quality, bugs in open-source repos
   - "Snake eating its own tail": AI now trained on AI-generated code → progressive quality degradation → "baseline slop"

3. **Code defects:**
   - Even popular languages have "subtle but serious defects"
   - Deprecated syntax, outdated libraries, security holes

4. **Context window issues:**
   - Too small: Can't load entire large repositories → hallucinations
   - Too large: Adds noise, distracts attention mechanism → confusion, decreased performance
   - Most handle small-to-medium repos, struggle with large existing projects

5. **Greenfield vs. Brownfield:**
   - Better at creating new (greenfield) code bases
   - Much less effective at refactoring existing (brownfield) code

6. **Semi-analog activities:**
   - Requirements gathering from stakeholders not digitized
   - System/ecosystem design only captured "at coarsest level in Visio diagrams"
   - These activities "not immediately available as training data"

**Bottom Line:**
- AI can generate "mostly acceptable" code "most of the time" for "most common languages" and "most functional use cases"
- Expects "incremental" improvement with more compute, training data, better algorithms
- Result: "Gradual narrowing of software engineer roles" - industry may need only "a fraction of engineers" employed today
- Roles will transform to focus on "human qualities like reasoning, logic, and intuition"
- This transformation already happened in aerospace and electrical engineering

### Q: What are the four AI coping strategies for engineers?
A:
**Strategy 1: Ride the Tiger** - Build AI frontier models
- Pros: Potentially very high salaries, "change human civilization itself"
- Cons: 
  - Extremely high competition
  - Extremely high education/training requirements
  - Unpredictability: AI bubble could collapse → "AI winter" → "completely out of a job in just a few years"

**Strategy 2: Outrun the Tiger** - Focus on very new technologies without training data
- Pros: High-paying jobs for a period
- Cons:
  - "Almost like gambling" - must pick technology that "goes viral"
  - Must correctly guess next technology when current one declines
  - "If you guess wrong, you're out of a job"
  - Engineers get slower at learning with age
  - "Progressively harder to keep changing technology stack"
  - "In long run, can't outrun the tiger" - only works short-term

**Strategy 3: Tame the Tiger** - Master AI tools, provide generalized intelligence
- Approach: Wear multiple hats (tech lead + architect + product owner + project manager)
- Use "army of AI agents" to do narrow grunt work
- Ship complex projects alone or with small team
- Can be job at company or own company/side hustle
- Pros: "Very productive and high-paying job potentially for a long time"
- Cons:
  - "Almost every other engineer is looking to do this"
  - Unique skillset for orchestration "not necessarily very common in engineers"
  - Many people (engineers, PMs, product owners) "may think they can do all these things but truly only a small subset have that broad breadth of skills"

**Strategy 4: Hide from the Tiger** - Work in industries hard to digitize
- Target: Highly regulated industries (defense, healthcare)
- Work either prevented from being digitized/shared or heavily dependent on "real analog world"
- Pros: Job stability "at least for a while"
- Cons: Over time, digital transformation → all industries/sectors eventually digitize → solutions become available as training data
- "Over long run, hiding strategy may not pan out"

**His Honest Assessment:**
- "I can't think of a scenario where everyone in the tech industry wins"
- "At least some of us may be able to find a niche and survive"
- "Take everything I'm saying with a giant grain of salt" - just a "laid-off ex big tech flunky"

### Q: How does the situation for junior engineers today compare to when he started?
A:
**Then (Early 2000s):**
- "Just showing sheepskin diploma was enough to get foot in the door"
- Joined consulting, knew nothing practical (no unit tests, mocking, debuggers, dependency management)
- Fixed bugs that failed in higher environments
- Incredibly supportive team (Dimma the build master, Naga the tech lead)
- First launch night: stayed until 5am fixing production bugs
- Had impostor syndrome but "burning desire to improve"
- Always had mentors and support network

**Now (2025):**
- "Way more engineers on job market" - "learn to code" initiatives created "huge saturation of engineering graduates"
- College degree worth "astronomically less than a generation ago"
- Must compete with:
  - Experienced mid-level engineers keeping jobs
  - Laid-off engineers willing to demote to entry-level
  - Offshore engineers working for lower wages
  - Senior engineers with AI productivity boost
  - "Data centers filled with GPUs for corporate budget"
- **Paradox**: Junior engineers today have "vastly superior knowledge and capability" compared to his cohort
- Example: Summer intern "architected, implemented, and deployed cloud-native web application" that company used - still didn't get hired
- "Less of a support network," "every incentive to discard them as soon as possible"

**His Verdict:**
- "Tech industry has evolved into this machine that is willingly wasting the potential of an entire generation of smart and talented engineers"
- "Feels kind of like an injustice"
- Personal stake: His teenage son is STEM-inclined, just pushed first PRs

### Q: What are the coping strategies for junior engineers trying to enter tech?
A:
**Strategy 1: Be True to Yourself**
- If you have passion/desire for CS or "just sort of like it" → keep doing it
- If you don't like it, despise it → reconsider
- Don't do it just for: parents, family expectations, "bonkers money," TikTok lifestyle videos
- "Don't betray that inner voice"
- Has met people who pursued CS for external reasons → "source of deep regret"

**Strategy 2: Build Your Eminence**
- NOT just personal portfolio websites you host yourself
- Work recognized by institutions, companies, organizations:
  - Build software for university or nonprofit organizations
  - Contribute meaningful software to free open-source frameworks
  - Join meetup groups, give lectures
  - Join and win hackathons
- "Demonstrations of eminence that will matter"

**Strategy 3: Maximize Value of Internships**
- "Not a guarantee of full-time offer"
- BUT "astronomically more likely to land full-time job than just cold applying"
- Apply through: school boards, career fairs, alumni networks, professional networks
- If no internship: try hardest to apply for jobs (see next strategy)

**Strategy 4: Apply Strategically for Jobs**
- Ensure resume is "professionally reviewed and polished"
- Practice LeetCode and HackerRank questions, "get good at it"
- Do mock interviews to get used to environment
- Apply to "broad range of companies" - not just tech companies but also non-tech companies
- Look for "technical software engineering role" - can exist in non-tech companies
- "Technical job is what's important for building experience"

**Strategy 5: Consider Adjacent Roles**
- If no hits for software engineering, apply to adjacent roles:
  - QA engineer
  - Data engineer
  - Sales engineer
  - DevOps engineer
  - Others
- Once in adjacent role, "gradually move into software engineering field"

**Strategy 6: Be Flexible**
- If can't get full-time tech job with big tech, consider:
  - Partnerships
  - Gigs
  - Solo entrepreneurship
  - Starting own project
  - Moving to different location or different country
- "Be flexible. Be like water, my friends."

**Promise:**
- "I make no promises, but I believe for most of you, if you try these tactics, it'll move the needle"
