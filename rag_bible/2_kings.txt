# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Kings: Introduction Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry opens his commentary on 2 Kings by emphasizing the book's tragic trajectory from glory to desolation. He highlights the central theme that **"a kingdom divided against itself cometh to destruction,"** tracing how Israel's division ultimately led to the captivity of both kingdoms. 

Henry identifies a key theological pattern throughout the narrative: **the supremacy of prophetic ministry over political power**. He notes that "these prophets out-shone their princes," suggesting that God's spiritual representatives held greater significance than earthly rulers in the divine economy.

The commentary frames the entire book around the principle of divine justice, pointing to how national righteousness and wickedness directly correlate with blessing and judgment.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws a fundamental lesson about national morality, concluding that **"this history, in the several passages of it, confirms that observation of Solomon, That righteousness exalts a nation, but sin is the reproach of any people."** This serves as his overarching practical application—that both individuals and nations reap the consequences of their moral choices.

The emphasis on prophetic ministry suggests practical lessons about the importance of spiritual leadership and God's word taking precedence over political considerations.

## Structure and Flow

Henry organizes 2 Kings into eight major sections, creating a clear narrative arc:
- **Elijah's dramatic conclusion** (fire from heaven and ascension)
- **Elisha's extensive miracle ministry** serving both Israelites and foreigners
- **Divine judgment through Hazael and Jehu** against Ahab's house and Baal worship
- **The reigns of various kings** in both kingdoms
- **The ten tribes' captivity**
- **Hezekiah's exemplary reign**
- **The contrast between Manasseh's wickedness and Josiah's reforms**
- **Jerusalem's final destruction** under Babylon

## Historical Context

Henry notes the book's continuity with 1 Kings, suggesting it could have begun with Ahaziah's reign in the previous book's final verse. He positions 2 Kings as completing the narrative of Israel's decline, contrasting sharply with the "illustrious beginning" of the united kingdom in 1 Kings.

The commentary presents this as a cautionary tale about the inevitable consequences of abandoning covenant faithfulness, while simultaneously celebrating God's continued work through his prophetic servants even in dark times.

# Summary: Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 1

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **divine sovereignty versus human rebellion** as the central theme. When Ahaziah "rebelled against God, and revolted from his allegiance to him, Moab rebelled against Israel," demonstrating how spiritual unfaithfulness leads to temporal consequences. The commentary stresses that **practical atheism** underlies idolatry - Elijah's rebuke suggests Ahaziah's consultation of Baal-zebub implies "there is no God at all in Israel."

Henry interprets the fire from heaven as divine vindication of prophetic authority, showing "what an interest the prophets had in heaven" and conversely "what an interest heaven had in the prophets." He emphasizes God's protection of His messengers: "one Elijah is more to God than 10,000 captains and their fifties."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications about **spiritual priorities in crisis**. Rather than asking "Shall I recover?" believers should ask "What means may I use that I may recover?" and more importantly, "how we may conduct ourselves well in our sickness, and get good to our souls by it." 

He warns against **consulting ungodly sources** for guidance: "It is a very wicked thing, upon any occasion or pretence whatsoever, to consult with the devil." When facing difficulties, believers should examine their own covenant-breaking: "If men break their covenants with us...we must reflect upon our breach of covenant with God."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On Ahaziah's fall (v.2), Henry observes: "Whatever we go, there is but a step between us and death. A man's house is his castle, but not to secure him against the judgments of God." Royal palaces provide no immunity from divine judgment.

Regarding Baal-zebub (v.2), Henry notes this "dunghill-deity" may have gained fame for supposedly ridding the land of flies or giving answers "with a humming noise, like that of a great fly." He connects this to Christ's era when "the prince of the devils is called Beel-zebub."

Henry emphasizes Elijah's humble appearance (v.8) - his "hairy garment" and "leathern girdle" showed that "He that was clothed with the Spirit despised all rich and gay clothing," paralleling John the Baptist.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively cross-references Scripture throughout. He connects Ahaziah's fate to his father Ahab, referencing how "the bow drawn at a venture" killed Ahab, while "the cracked lattice" proves fatal to the son. He links the fire from heaven to Elijah's previous miracle (1 Kings 17:38), contrasting God's acceptance of sacrifice with judgment on sinners.

He references Job 18:9-10 regarding divine judgment, Zechariah 1:6 about God's enduring word, and notably Luke 9:54 where Christ forbade the disciples from following Elijah's precedent of calling down fire, showing how the New Testament era brought different standards for dealing with opposition.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 2 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Elijah's translation as profoundly symbolic, serving multiple theological purposes. He sees God's decision to translate Elijah as: (1) a reward for "eminent and extraordinary" past services, encouraging future prophets to show similar zeal; (2) providing "sensible proof of another life after this" during the church's "dark and degenerate state"; and (3) offering "a type and figure of the ascension of Christ and the opening of the kingdom of heaven to all believers."

The commentary emphasizes the contrast between earthly and heavenly departures, noting that "men are as their end is" - comparing the disgraceful death of a wicked king with Elijah's glorious translation. Henry views this as demonstrating God's sovereign choice in honoring His faithful servants.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Faithful Discipleship**: Elisha's persistence in following Elijah despite three attempts to dissuade him exemplifies dedication. Henry warns: "Let not those that follow Christ come short by tiring at last."

**Preparing for Loss**: When the sons of the prophets warned Elisha about losing his master, Henry applies this universally: "Know we not that our nearest relations, and dearest friends, must shortly be taken from us?" He urges believers to "carefully do the duty of every relation" while we can.

**Spiritual Ambition**: Elisha's request for a "double portion" represents "holy ambition to covet earnestly the best gifts, and those which will render us most serviceable to God and our brethren."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-8**: Henry emphasizes the schools of the prophets as God's gracious provision during apostasy, noting that "Much of God was among these prophets" even in places like Bethel where golden calves stood. The Jordan crossing parallels both Moses and Joshua, with Elijah's mantle serving as "an equivalent token of God's presence" for those without the ark.

**Verses 9-12**: Henry interprets Elijah's offer ("Ask what I shall do for thee") as earthly assistance only, observing that "we know not that we can have access to any friend in heaven but Christ." The chariot of fire represents divine honor for one "despised and rejected of men."

**Verses 13-25**: Elisha's miracles immediately establish his prophetic authority, with Henry noting that "This revolution in prophecy makes a greater figure than the revolution of a kingdom."

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects Elijah's translation to Enoch's (Genesis 5:24) and Christ's ascension, seeing both as previews of believers' future glorification. The Jordan crossing recalls Joshua 3:7 and Israel's entrance into Canaan. 

He references John 16:6 regarding the disciples' sorrow at Christ's departure, paralleling Elisha's grief. The healing of Jericho's waters echoes Moses' healing of Marah's bitter waters. Henry also connects Christ's promise to send the Spirit with Elijah's blessing of Elisha.

The commentary emphasizes that Elijah's departure, like Christ's, involved blessing and empowering successors to continue the work. Henry sees the entire narrative as demonstrating how "God graciously made up" for institutional religious failures through extraordinary prophetic ministry.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty in judgment and deliverance. He presents King Jehoram as representing incomplete reformation—removing some idols while maintaining others for political convenience. Henry notes that "those do not truly, nor acceptably, repent or reform, who only part with the sins that they lose by, but continue their affection to the sins that they get by."

The commentary highlights God's patience and provision even for imperfect leaders. Despite Jehoram's partial reforms, divine judgment remained inevitable for Ahab's house, demonstrating that superficial changes cannot avert God's justice when "the measure of iniquity was full."

## Key Practical Applications

**On Incomplete Reformation:** Henry warns against selective obedience, noting Jehoram "only put away the image of Baal" but "adhered to the worship of the calves." This teaches that true repentance requires abandoning all sin, not just what's socially or politically expedient.

**On Seeking Divine Guidance:** Henry criticizes waiting until crisis to seek God's counsel. Regarding Jehoshaphat's belated inquiry for a prophet, he observes: "It was well that Jehoshaphat enquired of the Lord now, but it would have been much better if he had done it sooner."

**On Unequal Alliances:** Henry warns about "being yoked with unbelievers," noting how Jehoshaphat's alliance with Ahab's family repeatedly brought danger.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3:** Henry sees Jehoram as "not so bad, so overmuch wicked" as his parents, yet emphasizes his reformation was "next to none" because it was incomplete and politically motivated.

**Verse 11:** Henry marvels that Elisha followed the army "as a volunteer, unasked, unobserved," demonstrating how "God anticipate[s] his people with the blessings of his goodness."

**Verse 13:** Elisha's rebuke to Jehoram—"Get thee to the prophets of thy father and mother"—exemplifies prophetic boldness in confronting compromise.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage extensively to other texts: Ecclesiastes 7:17 regarding not being "overmuch wicked," Proverbs 19:3 about man's foolishness perverting his way, Numbers 20:2 recalling Israel's earlier water shortage in the wilderness, and Judges 10:14 about seeking help from the gods one has served.

Henry concludes that God's providence orchestrates both judgment and mercy, using even human improvidence to display divine wisdom and power in deliverance.

# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 4

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes that Elisha's five miracles demonstrate **divine power combined with practical goodness**. Unlike seeking earthly honor for his service to the three kings, Elisha "preferred the honour of doing good in the schools of the prophets before that of being great in the courts of princes." Henry notes that "Elisha's miracles were for use, not for show" - they were "not only great wonders, but great favours" that revealed how "God magnifies his goodness with his power."

A central theme is **God's providential care for the faithful**. The prophet's widow could depend "upon the promise that the seed of the righteous shall not be forsaken," while God proves himself "a husband" to widows and "a father" to the fatherless. Henry sees divine blessing as working through **human diligence and faith** - the oil multiplied through the widow's own pouring, teaching that "it is in connexion with our own careful and diligent endeavours that we may expect the blessing of God."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian living:

**On Charity and Debt:** True charity helps people become self-sufficient rather than merely providing temporary relief. The best approach is "to help them into a way of improving what little they have by their own industry and ingenuity." Regarding debt, Henry emphasizes that "It is one of the fundamental laws of our religion that we render to all their due" - paying debts must take priority even over family provision.

**On Stewardship:** The oil miracle teaches that "the way to increase what we have is to use it" - it's "not hoarding the talents, but trading with them, that doubles them." Henry notes we are "never straitened in God...all our straitness is in ourselves. It is our faith that fails, not his promise."

**On Hospitality:** The Shunammite woman's example shows how **genuine discernment and generous hospitality** toward God's servants brings divine blessing.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-7 (The Widow's Oil):** Henry emphasizes that the prophet died poor despite being godly, noting "the children of light are not always wise for this world." The miracle required the widow's active participation - she had to pour the oil herself, demonstrating that divine blessing works through human effort.

**Verses 8-17 (The Shunammite's Son):** Henry highlights the woman's wisdom in recognizing Elisha as "a holy man of God" and her contentment when offered royal favor: "I dwell among mine own people." Her initial disbelief at the promise of a son ("do not lie unto thine handmaid") shows the magnitude of God's unexpected blessing.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the widow's situation to **Ecclesiastes 9:15** regarding how "the wise man delivered the army, but no man remembered the wise man." He references the **bondservant law of Exodus 21:2**, Job's metaphor of rocks pouring out oil (**Job 29:6**), and **Asher's blessing in Deuteronomy 33:24** about dipping his foot in oil.

The miracle of multiplication recalls **Zechariah 4:12** about golden oil flowing from the olive tree, while the promised son parallels God's promises to **Abraham, Isaac, Manoah, and Elkanah**. Henry also connects the quality of the miraculous oil to **John 2:10** regarding the superior wine at Cana.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry interprets Naaman's healing as a powerful type of **God's grace to the Gentiles**, drawing from Christ's reference in Luke 4:27. He contrasts this with Gehazi's punishment, viewing it as symbolic of "the blinding and rejecting of the Jews, who envied God's grace to the Gentiles." The commentary emphasizes God's universal mercy: "God does good to all, and will have all men to be saved."

A central theme is **divine sovereignty over human affairs**. Henry notes that even Syria's military victories came from the Lord: "The preservation and prosperity even of those that do not know God and serve him must be ascribed to him, for he is the Saviour of all men."

## Key Practical Applications

**Humility and Obedience**: Henry emphasizes that "those that will be helped of God must do as they are bidden." Naaman's initial pride in expecting elaborate ceremonies teaches that God often works through simple means to humble human pride.

**Servants as Witnesses**: The commentary highlights how the captive Israelite maid became a blessing to Naaman's household. Henry observes: "Servants may be blessings to the families where they are, by telling what they know of the glory of God and the honour of his prophets."

**Universal Human Condition**: Despite Naaman's greatness, Henry notes that "every man has some but or other in his character...some allay to his grandeur." This teaches that worldly success cannot shield us from life's fundamental struggles.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verse 1**, Henry observes the irony that this mighty general "was a leper," noting that "the basest slave in Syria would not change skins with him."

Regarding **verses 11-12**, Henry explains Naaman's anger stemmed from expecting elaborate rituals instead of simple obedience: "He had been fancying how this cure would be wrought" with ceremonial prayers and hand-waving over the diseased area.

On **verse 14**, Henry emphasizes the complete restoration: "his flesh came again like unto the flesh of a little child" - highlighting God's power to make all things new.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects this narrative to **Luke 4:27** where Christ references Naaman as the only leper cleansed in Israel during Elisha's time. He also references **Acts 8:4** regarding how "the unhappy dispersing of the people of God has sometimes proved the happy occasion of the diffusion of the knowledge of God."

The commentary draws parallels to **Psalm 8:2** about children declaring God's works and **Jeremiah 29:7** concerning seeking peace even in captivity. Henry sees in Naaman's story a foreshadowing of the gospel going to the Gentiles while Israel often rejected it.

The narrative ultimately demonstrates that spiritual cleansing, like physical healing, requires humble submission to God's prescribed methods, regardless of our expectations or social status.

Chapter 6
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 6: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **God's sovereignty over nature and nations** through Elisha's miracles. The iron swimming demonstrates that "the God of nature is not tied up to its laws," while God's omniscience is highlighted through divine revelation of Syrian war plans. Henry sees God's **protective care for His servants**, noting that "they that be with us are more than they that be with them" - referring to heavenly hosts surrounding believers.

The commentary reveals **God's grace transforming hearts**, as Henry spiritualizes the floating iron: "God's grace can thus raise the stony iron heart which has sunk into the mud of this world, and raise up affections naturally earthly, to things above."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from the sons of the prophets:

**Contentment and Industry**: They were "humble men" who didn't "affect that which was gay or great," content with "mean things" while being "industrious" and "willing to take pains." Henry warns against being "idle drones" and emphasizes honest employment.

**Integrity and Stewardship**: The borrowed axe incident teaches that "we ought to be as careful of that which is borrowed as of that which is our own." Henry notes the prophet's honest concern: "Alas, master! For it was borrowed."

**Wisdom in Leadership**: Ministers shouldn't think themselves "too great to stoop to do good" and should seek counsel from those "wiser and more experienced."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-7 (The Floating Axe)**: Henry portrays the prophetic community as growing ("many are added to them"), humble, poor, and industrious. Elisha's miracle demonstrates both his compassion and divine power.

**Verses 8-12 (Divine Intelligence)**: Henry emphasizes how "all those devices are known to God, even those that are deepest laid" and that God's prophets warn of dangers both temporal and eternal.

**Verses 16-17 (Spiritual Vision)**: The famous "Fear not" passage illustrates how God opens spiritual eyes to see heavenly protection, countering natural fear with supernatural reality.

**Verses 22-23 (Gracious Treatment)**: Elisha's mercy toward enemies demonstrates Christian ethics - treating captives with kindness rather than revenge.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the narrative to broader biblical themes through references to **2 Thessalonians 3:8,10** (regarding honest work), **Nehemiah 4:11** (enemies' secret plotting), and parallels to Elijah's encounters with Syrian captains. He implicitly references Jesus' teachings on loving enemies through Elisha's merciful treatment of the Syrian soldiers.

The commentary presents Elisha as "still a great blessing both to church and state," serving as a type of divine grace that protects, provides, and transforms hearts from earthly to heavenly affections.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 7: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies God's sovereignty and timing as central themes, emphasizing that "Man's extremity is God's opportunity of magnifying his own power." The commentary highlights how God delivers His people precisely when circumstances appear most desperate, demonstrating that divine intervention often comes through unexpected means. The theological contrast between faith and unbelief runs throughout, with the unbelieving lord serving as a warning that "Unbelief is a sin by which men greatly dishonour and displease God, and deprive themselves of the favours he designed for them."

Henry notes how God works through the marginalized—four lepers become the instruments of deliverance—showing that divine providence employs unlikely vessels. The commentary emphasizes God's control over human senses, noting how "God knows how to work upon every sense" as He caused the Syrians to hear phantom armies.

## Key Practical Applications

The lepers' reasoning provides a model for desperate situations: "better die by the sword than by famine." Henry draws parallels to the prodigal son, showing how prudence leads us toward hope rather than despair. Their initial selfishness followed by conscience-driven sharing demonstrates that "the most comfortable advantage is that which our brethren share with us in."

Henry warns against the king's suspicious nature, suggesting that unbelief makes us expect deception even when God provides deliverance. The commentary advocates for patient faith over anxious questioning of divine timing and methods.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Elisha's prophecy comes at the moment of deepest despair, fulfilling the principle that God appears "when their strength is gone" (Deuteronomy 32:36). The unbelieving lord's demand for miraculous signs—"windows in heaven"—reveals how skepticism blinds us to God's power.

**Verses 6-7**: Henry notes the irony that "notices from the invisible world are either very comfortable or very dreadful, according as men are at peace with God." The Syrians' panic demonstrates that "the wicked flee when none pursues."

**Verse 9**: The lepers' conviction that "We do not well" shows how conscience corrects selfish impulses toward community responsibility.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this account to multiple biblical parallels: Deuteronomy 32:36 (God's timing), Luke 18:8 (finding faith), Judges 5:11 (gates reopening in peace), and Exodus 4:11 (God's control over senses). He compares the lepers to the prodigal son and references the murmuring Israelites who saw Canaan but couldn't enter due to unbelief. The phantom army recalls Elijah's servant seeing heavenly horses and chariots (2 Kings 6:17).

Henry draws eschatological parallels, suggesting the unbelieving lord's fate foreshadows those who "believe not the promise of eternal life; they shall see it at a distance—Abraham afar off, but shall never taste of it." This connects earthly unbelief to eternal consequences, making the historical account a spiritual warning for all generations.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 8 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's sovereignty in judgment and providence throughout this chapter. He views the seven-year famine as divine punishment for Israel's persistent wickedness, noting that "when he judgeth he will overcome." The commentary highlights how God's judgments escalate when lesser ones fail to produce repentance - this famine lasted twice as long as Elijah's drought because "if men will walk contrary to him, he will heat the furnace yet hotter."

A key theme is God's faithful care for the righteous. Henry sees the Shunammite's preservation as evidence that God rewards those who show kindness to His servants, demonstrating that "the kindness of the good Shunammite to the prophet [was] rewarded by the care that was taken of her."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Foresight and Wisdom**: He praises the Shunammite's early departure, noting "It is our happiness to foresee an evil, and our wisdom, when we foresee it, to hide ourselves." This teaches believers to heed prophetic warnings.

**Divine Timing**: The providential timing of the woman's petition coinciding with the king's conversation about Elisha demonstrates that "Providence is to be acknowledged in ordering the circumstances of events."

**Authority's Responsibility**: Henry emphasizes that rulers must actively protect the wronged: "It is not enough for those in authority that they do no wrong themselves, but they must support the right of those that are wronged."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Regarding Elisha's cryptic response to Hazael about Ben-hadad's recovery (v. 10), Henry explains the distinction between natural recovery from illness versus actual death by murder. 

On Hazael's shock at the prophecy of his future cruelty (v. 13), Henry observes how "affliction brings those to God who in their prosperity had made light of him," noting the irony that Ben-hadad sought Israel's God while Israel's own king had previously consulted foreign gods.

Henry finds it remarkable that Ben-hadad honored Elisha with such an elaborate forty-camel gift and sent his prime minister, showing "it is no disparagement to the greatest of men to attend the prophets of the Lord."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this chapter extensively to other biblical passages:
- Links the famine to Joel 1:3-4 and God's covenant curses in the law
- References Amos 3:2 regarding God's special judgment on His chosen people
- Connects to 1 Kings 19:15 regarding Hazael's earlier designation as king
- Cites Proverbs 25:19 and Micah 7:5 about the unreliability of human help
- References Psalm 82:3-4 and Proverbs 31:9 on rulers' duty to protect the oppressed

The commentary masterfully weaves together themes of divine sovereignty, human responsibility, and God's faithful care for the righteous while warning of escalating judgment for persistent rebellion.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 9 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as the fulfillment of divine justice against the house of Ahab. The central theological theme is **God's sovereignty in executing judgment** through human instruments. Henry emphasizes that Jehu's commission came "from a higher king to fight against" his earthly master, illustrating how divine authority supersedes human loyalty when God's justice demands action.

The commentary stresses that **persecution of God's servants brings certain judgment**. Henry notes that while idolatry was serious, "the controversy God has with them is for their being persecutors" - specifically "the blood of God's servants, the prophets and others, faithful worshippers, which they had shed." He warns that "nothing fills the measure of the iniquity of any prince or people as this does nor brings a surer or a sorer ruin."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for Christian life and leadership:

**For those in authority**: Magistrates must remember they are "ministers of God" who "must act in dependence upon him and with an entire devotedness to him and to his glory." Leaders should view their people not as personal vassals but as "God's freemen, his sons, his first-born."

**For servants of God**: The young prophet's example teaches that God's messengers should "be quick and lively at their work" and "should be as angels that fly swiftly." Henry emphasizes that "those whom God sends on his errands shall not go without full instructions."

**For all believers**: The commentary warns against the world's contempt for genuine faith, noting how "those that have no religion commonly speak with disdain of those that are religious, and look upon them as mad."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich detail on Jehu's anointing, explaining why Elisha sent a young prophet rather than going himself - it involved danger, required physical strength, yet offered honor to encourage the young prophets. He notes the private nature of the anointing tested "Jehu's confidence in his commission" when "he had no witness to attest it."

The commentary emphasizes that Jehu was to destroy Ahab's house "not that he might clear his own way to the throne" but to "execute the judgments of God." Henry observes that God's command would "justify thee and bear thee out" - removing both fear of sin and danger.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this narrative to broader biblical patterns, referencing David's earlier anointing (1 Samuel 16:2), Solomon's anointing (1 Kings 1:39), and Joash's coronation (2 Kings 11:12). He links the persecution theme to Jerusalem's destruction (2 Chronicles 36:16) and Christ's prophecy (Matthew 23:37-38).

The commentary also references how the world viewed other biblical figures as "mad" - Christ ("beside himself"), John Baptist ("has a devil"), and Paul ("much learning has made him mad") - demonstrating the consistent pattern of worldly contempt for godliness.

Henry concludes that "the highest wisdom is thus represented as folly, and those that best understand themselves are looked upon as beside themselves," showing how this chapter's themes resonate throughout Scripture.

Chapter 10
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 10: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents 2 Kings 10 as a demonstration of **divine judgment and the fulfillment of prophetic word**. The central theological theme is God's sovereignty in executing justice through human agents, even when their motives are mixed. Henry emphasizes that "there shall fall unto the earth nothing of the word of the LORD, which the LORD spake concerning the house of Ahab" (v. 10), showing God's faithfulness to His pronouncements through Elijah.

Henry wrestles with the moral complexity of Jehu's actions, noting that while the executions fulfilled divine justice, Jehu's methods revealed character flaws. He observes that "God is not the author of any man's sin, but even by that which men do from bad principles God serves his own purposes."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**On submission to divine authority**: When the rulers feared Jehu, Henry notes they reasoned, "Behold, two kings stood not before him...how then shall we stand?" He applies this: "With much more reason may we thus argue ourselves into a subjection to the great God...we must either bend or break."

**On the danger of compromised loyalty**: Observing how Ahab's former supporters betrayed his sons, Henry warns: "Learn hence not to trust in a friend...not governed by conscience. One can scarcely expect that he who has been false to his God should ever be faithful to his prince."

**On the consequences of tyranny**: Henry notes divine irony in how the same elders who obeyed Jezebel's murderous commands against Naboth now readily executed her family, warning that "Princes that make their people slaves take the readiest way to make them rebels."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-10**: Henry sees God's hand in making "their guardians their murderers," noting the providential irony that seventy sons—"such a quiver full of arrows"—could not protect Ahab's house from divine vengeance.

**Verses 15-16**: On Jehu's encounter with Jehonadab, Henry appreciates the simple test of fellowship: "Is thine heart right, as my heart is with thy heart?" He admires Jehonadab as a godly man who, though "mortified to the world," supported righteous causes.

**Verses 18-28**: Henry praises Jehu's "holy craft" in destroying Baal worship, noting the irony that "Ahab served Baal a little; but Jehu shall serve him much"—meaning to destroy rather than worship.

**Verses 29-31**: Henry identifies Jehu's critical failure: retaining the golden calf worship. This "old idolatry" showed that Jehu's zeal was more political than purely religious.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects the narrative to broader scriptural themes:

- Links to **Judges 8:30** regarding Gideon's seventy sons, showing how numerous families can quickly perish
- References **Jeremiah 48:10** about not doing God's work "deceitfully"
- Connects to **Isaiah 34:5-6** regarding God's final judgment: "Then his eye will not spare, neither will he pity"
- Draws parallels to **2 Chronicles 22** for additional details on Ahaziah's relatives
- References **Jeremiah 35** regarding Jehonadab's descendants and their faithfulness

Henry concludes that while Jehu accomplished God's immediate purposes in judgment, his incomplete reformation—maintaining the calf worship—led to continued divine chastisement through Hazael. The chapter serves as both a testimony to divine justice and a warning about half-hearted obedience to God.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 11: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**God's Covenant Faithfulness**: Henry emphasizes that "God had assured David of the continuance of his family, which is called his ordaining a lamp for his anointed." Despite Athaliah's murderous rampage, God's promise to David remained unbreakable—"David's promised lamp almost extinguished and yet wonderfully preserved." This demonstrates that "who can disannul what God hath purposed?"

**Divine Providence in Preservation**: The rescue of baby Joash represents God's sovereign protection of His covenant line. Henry notes this was "a brand plucked out of the fire" and connects it to Psalm 27:5: "In the secret of his tabernacle shall he hide me." The temple became Joash's sanctuary, making David's words literally true for his descendant.

**Spiritual Typology**: Henry sees the hidden heir as representing "the sons and heirs of heaven" who "are now hidden, the world knows them not (1 John 3:1), yet the time is fixed when they shall appear in glory, as Joash in his seventh year."

## Key Practical Applications

**Marriage Wisdom**: Henry highlights the providential importance of godly marriages, noting that Joram's decision to marry his daughter to priest Jehoiada "proved a happy marriage, and the saving of the royal family from ruin." He concludes: "see what blessings those lay up in store for their families that marry their children to those that are wise and good."

**Leadership and Order**: Jehoiada's careful planning demonstrates wise leadership. Henry praises how "good order contributes very much to the expediting and accomplishing of any great enterprise. Let every man know, and keep, and make good, his post, and then the work will be done quickly."

**Balancing Priorities**: Even during political revolution, Jehoiada maintained religious priorities, ensuring temple protocols were preserved: "He was not so zealous for the projected revolution as to forget his religion."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: Henry describes Athaliah's massacre as driven by "ambition" and "revenge and rage against God." Her attempt to destroy David's line was "a foolish attempt and fruitless" against divine promises.

**Verses 4-12**: The coronation ceremony receives detailed treatment. Henry notes the symbolic elements: the crown (kingly power), the testimony/Bible (governing by God's law), and anointing (receiving the Spirit for leadership).

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves multiple scriptural connections throughout:
- 2 Chronicles 21:4, 22:1,8 (royal bloodshed context)
- Psalm 27:5 (God hiding His people)
- 1 John 3:1 (hidden sons of heaven)
- Deuteronomy 17:12,18-19 (priestly authority and royal obligations)
- Ecclesiastes 10:17 (noble leadership)

The commentary masterfully connects this historical narrative to broader themes of covenant faithfulness, divine providence, and Christ's ultimate kingship through David's line.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Kings 12: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes the critical importance of godly mentorship and spiritual direction. Joash "did that which was right in the sight of the LORD all his days wherein Jehoiada the priest instructed him," but this righteousness was conditional and temporary. The commentary reveals a central theme of incomplete reformation—while Joash showed zeal for temple repairs, "the high places were not taken away," indicating partial obedience that would later prove spiritually fatal.

Henry draws attention to the tragedy of wasted potential: Joash was "not so illustrious at forty years old as he was at seven," demonstrating how early promise can deteriorate without sustained spiritual guidance. The temple repair project becomes a metaphor for spiritual stewardship and the responsibility of leaders to maintain God's house.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry offers pointed counsel for young people and leaders: "It is a great mercy to young people, and especially to young princes, and all young men of consequence, to be under good direction." He emphasizes that wisdom lies in remaining teachable and "willing to be counselled and ruled by" godly advisors even into adulthood.

Regarding church work and public service, Henry notes that "Church work is usually slow work, but it is a pity that churchmen, of all men, should be slow at it." He advocates for faithful stewardship, observing that "when public distributions are made faithfully public contributions will be made cheerfully." The commentary stresses practical priorities: "give that the preference which is most needful" rather than focusing on ornamental additions.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On the temple repairs, Henry observes that the initial method failed because either "the priests were careless" or "the people had so little confidence in the priests' management." This led to implementing a transparent public chest system that restored donor confidence.

Henry notes the wisdom of employing specialists: rather than using priests who "were not versed in affairs of this nature," the work was given to "those that did the work, or at least had the oversight of it." The workers gained such a reputation that "there was no occasion to examine their bills or audit their accounts."

Henry references 2 Chronicles 24:7 to explain the temple's deteriorated condition, noting that "the sons of Athaliah had broken up the house of God." He connects the temple repair fund to Leviticus 27:2-3 regarding redemption money and references Matthew 21:33 regarding unfaithful stewards.

The commentary alludes to Jesus's teaching about charitable giving ("Let not thy left hand know what thy right hand doeth") in discussing the chest's placement. Henry also references Zephaniah 3:18 regarding those who "make the reproach of them their burden" in relation to religious assemblies.

The work concludes with Joash's tragic end through conspiracy and assassination, fulfilling the pattern of incomplete reformation leading to judgment—a theme Henry sees repeated throughout the chronicles of Judah's kings.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 2 Kings Chapter 13

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents two central theological themes in this chapter. First, **God's covenant faithfulness despite human unfaithfulness** - even when Israel persistently worshipped the golden calves of Jeroboam and faced divine judgment through Syrian oppression, God still heard Jehoahaz's prayers "in remembrance of his covenant with Abraham" (v. 23). Regarding God's mercy, Henry writes that this should "invite and engage us for ever to him, and encourage even those that have forsaken him to return and repent; for there is forgiveness with him."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 4-5**: Henry notes that though Jehoahaz's prayer might "justly have been rejected as an abomination," God still provided "a saviour" - not Jehoahaz himself, but his son Joash who would defeat Syria.

**Verse 14**: On Elisha's sickness, Henry observes that despite having "the spirit of Elijah," Elisha "went the common road out of the world" rather than being taken up like Elijah, demonstrating that "if God honour some above others, who yet are not inferior to them in gifts or graces, who shall find fault?"

**Verses 18-19**: Henry emphasizes how Joash's halfhearted striking of arrows limited his victories - he would defeat Syria only three times instead of completely consuming them.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry references **Deuteronomy 4:7** regarding Israel's ancient honor of having "God nigh unto them," **Leviticus 26:42** about God's covenant remembrance, and **Proverbs 11:25** about how "he that watereth shall be watered also himself" when discussing how honoring previous generations brings similar honor in return.

The commentary consistently connects this chapter to the broader biblical narrative of covenant, judgment, and mercy that runs throughout Scripture.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 14 Summary

Henry emphasizes the tension between human pride and divine justice throughout this chapter. He identifies **pride as the root of all sin**, noting that "the heart must bear the blame" when rulers fall into presumption. The commentary highlights God's resistance to the proud, showing how Amaziah's success against Edom inflated his ego and led to his downfall against Israel.

A central theme is **progressive spiritual decline**. While Amaziah "did that which was right in the sight of the LORD," Henry observes he fell short of David's standard. The commentary warns that "It is not enough to do that which our pious predecessors did, merely to keep up the usage, but we must do it from the same principle of faith and devotion."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for rulers and believers:

**Contentment vs. Ambition**: The commentary strongly emphasizes knowing one's limits. Regarding Amaziah's challenge to Israel, Henry notes: "Many would have had wealth and honour enough if they had but known when they had enough." This speaks to the danger of overreaching after initial success.

**Legal Restraint**: Henry praises Amaziah for sparing the murderers' children according to Mosaic law, showing that even in matters of justice, rulers must submit to God's revealed standards.

**Verses 3-4**: Henry notes the persistent problem of high places, observing that "It is hard to get clear of those corruptions which, by long usage, have gained both prescription and a favourable opinion."

**Verses 8-10**: Henry provides rich commentary on Joash's parable of the thistle and cedar, interpreting it as a masterful rebuke that "mortifies [Amaziah's] pride" while warning of consequences.

**Verse 14**: The commentary notes the irony that despite their different spiritual standings, the "evil" Joash triumphed over the "righteous" Amaziah, suggesting God's judgment on pride transcends relative righteousness.

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry connects Amaziah's story to broader scriptural principles, citing **Deuteronomy 24:16** regarding children not bearing their fathers' guilt, and **Proverbs 20:3** about avoiding unnecessary conflicts. He references **2 Chronicles 25** for additional details on the Edomite campaign.

The commentary links Amaziah's treatment of God's prophets to his defeat, noting that while Joash had honored Elisha, Amaziah had "been abusive to another" prophet, demonstrating that "God will honour those who honour him in his prophets."

Henry concludes with the sobering observation that "How many great men are made to stand in a little compass in God's book!" emphasizing the brevity of human achievement against eternal judgment.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 15: Summary

## Main Theological Themes

**Divine Judgment and Justice**: Henry emphasizes how God fulfills both His promises and warnings with precision. The chapter demonstrates that "God made good his promise to Jehu" regarding four generations ruling Israel, while simultaneously showing divine judgment on persistent sin. ## Key Practical Applications

**Leadership and Humility**: Henry warns that leaders who "presumptuously intruded into God's temple" will face consequences. Azariah's isolation serves as a perpetual reminder that "though we have reason to think he repented and the sin was pardoned, yet, for warning to others, he was continued under this mark of God's displeasure."

**National Unity**: Henry emphasizes how "intestine broils and contests for the sovereignty" make nations vulnerable to external enemies. He concludes: "Happy the land where that is settled," highlighting the importance of stable governance.

**The Danger of Ambition**: The commentary warns against leaders who "care not how much the welfare and repose of their country are sacrificed to their revenge and affectation of dominion."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Azariah's Leprosy (v. 5)**: Henry explains this as divine chastisement for "presumptuous invasion of the priests' office," demonstrating that "the greatest of men are not only subject to the common calamities, but also to the common infirmities, of human nature."

**The Succession of Violence**: Henry observes that each king (except Menahem) "conspired against his predecessor, and slew him," noting that "blood shall have blood, and he that dealt treacherously shalt be dealt treacherously with."

**Persistent Idolatry**: Despite political upheaval, Henry notes sadly that all these kings maintained "the sins of Jeroboam the son of Nebat," showing how "though they were emptied from vessel to vessel, that taste remained in them, and that scent was not changed."

## Cross-References

Henry connects this chapter to several biblical passages:
- **Leviticus 26:21** regarding escalating judgments for continued disobedience

- **Hosea 5:7** possibly referring to Shallum's brief month-long reign

The commentary demonstrates Henry's method of weaving together historical narrative with prophetic literature to show God's consistent character and dealings with His people across Scripture.

Henry concludes that this period of chaos could have been averted "if they had, in these days of confusion and perplexity, humbled themselves before God and sought his face," emphasizing repentance as the consistent biblical solution to divine judgment.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 17: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents the fall of Israel as a divine judgment executed through human instruments. He emphasizes that while the Assyrians were the immediate cause, **"It was the Lord that removed Israel out of his sight; whoever were the instruments, he was the author of this calamity."** The destruction fulfills prophetic warnings, demonstrating God's faithfulness to His word: **"Rather shall heaven and earth pass than one tittle of God's word fall to the ground."**

Henry addresses the theological tension of God destroying what He had miraculously established, concluding this was **"not purely an act of sovereignty"** but **"an act of necessary justice."** The captivity represents the culmination of generational sin, punishing not only current transgressions but **"the iniquities of their fathers, who had been long in filing the measure and treasuring up wrath."**

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several sobering lessons for contemporary believers:

**Gradual Divine Judgment**: God's punishment came incrementally—first tribute, then captivity—showing His patience and giving opportunities for repentance. **"If that less judgment had prevailed to humble and reform them, the greater would have been prevented."**

**The Futility of Human Solutions**: Instead of seeking God's help, Israel trusted Egypt for deliverance from Assyria. Henry notes that even if successful, this would have merely meant **"changing their oppressors."** **"Men get nothing by struggling with the net, but entangle themselves the more."**

**Corporate Responsibility**: Even under Hoshea, a relatively better king who **"gave them leave to do better, but they did as bad as ever,"** the people remained accountable for their choices, **"which laid the blame of their sin and ruin wholly upon themselves."**

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-6**: Henry observes the ironic contrast between Israel's entrance under Joshua ("Hoshea the son of Nun") and their tragic exit under the last king Hoshea ("son of Elah"), noting **"who would have thought that such as this should be their exit?"**

**Verses 7-18**: The historian's detailed catalog of Israel's sins demonstrates systematic rebellion against divine grace. Henry emphasizes their **"hardened necks, like to the neck of their fathers"** and how **"they followed vanity, and became vain."**

**Verse 23**: The fulfillment of prophetic warnings validates divine justice and biblical reliability.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive scriptural connections throughout his commentary:

- **Hosea 10:3** and **8:5** regarding Israel's rejection of proper authority and idolatry
- **Isaiah 10:5** describing Assyria as **"the rod of his anger"**
- **Isaiah 43:24** questioning **"Who gave Jacob for a spoil, and Israel to the robbers? Did not the Lord?"**
- **Revelation 7** and **James 1:1** suggesting remnants of the twelve tribes survived
- **Galatians 6:16** connecting Israel's name to **"the gospel church, the spiritual Israel"**

Henry concludes that while the ten tribes disappeared as a political entity, becoming **"Lo-ammi—not a people, and Lo-ruhamah—unpitied,"** their legacy continues in the spiritual Israel, where **"it will ever remain."** This provides both warning about divine justice and hope for redemptive purposes beyond immediate judgment.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 18: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes **divine grace overcoming human corruption** through Hezekiah's transformation. Despite wicked King Ahaz as his father, Hezekiah became "one of the best" kings, demonstrating that "what good there is in any is not of nature, but of grace, free grace, sovereign grace." This illustrates how God's grace "gets over the greatest difficulties and disadvantages."

The commentary highlights the **covenant faithfulness of God** even amid judgment. While Israel faced captivity for idolatry, Judah under Hezekiah showed "that it may appear God has not quite cast off the seed of Abraham" (Romans 11:1). Henry draws several practical lessons:

- **Immediate obedience in reform**: Hezekiah "began betimes" and "lost no time" in reformation, showing the importance of prompt action when called to righteousness.
- **Complete commitment over partial obedience**: Unlike previous kings who "did that which was right, but not like David," Hezekiah demonstrated full devotion.
- **Learning from others' judgment**: "It is well when our neighbours' harms are our warnings" - Israel's captivity should motivate Judah's reform.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 3-4**: Henry praises Hezekiah as "a second David" who had genuine "love for God's word, and God's house." His removal of high places showed he made "God's word his rule, not the example of his predecessors."

**Verse 4 (brazen serpent)**: Henry provides extended analysis of why Hezekiah destroyed this divinely-instituted object: "Good things, when idolized, are better parted with than kept." He called it "Nehushtan...nothing else but a piece of brass."

**Verse 5**: The phrase "none like him" refers specifically to his "courage and confidence in God" and "constancy and perseverance in his duty."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this chapter to multiple biblical passages:
- **Hosea 11:12** - Judah's faithfulness contrasted with Ephraim's deceit
- **Romans 11:1, 24** - God's preservation of Abraham's seed and sovereign grace
- **2 Chronicles 29:3** - Hezekiah's immediate reformation upon taking the throne
- **John 3:14** - The brazen serpent as a type of Christ
- **Isaiah 14:28** - Prophecy concerning Hezekiah's victories over Philistines

The commentary emphasizes how Hezekiah's story demonstrates God's faithfulness to His covenant people while warning against the dangers of idolatry that destroyed the northern kingdom.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 19: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry centers his commentary on God's sovereign deliverance of Jerusalem through divine intervention rather than human warfare. The chapter demonstrates God's supremacy over earthly powers and His faithfulness to those who trust Him. Henry emphasizes that this "glorious deliverance" came "not by sword or bow, but by prayer and prophecy, and by the hand of an angel."

A key theme is the contrast between blasphemous pride and humble faith. Sennacherib's arrogance in equating the living God with "deaf and dumb idols" becomes his downfall. Henry draws several practical lessons about prayer and crisis response:

Henry observes that Hezekiah honored both "God," "prayer," and his "brethren" by requesting Isaiah's intercession, while noting that "when we desire the prayers of others for us we must not think we are excused from praying for ourselves."

**On proper crisis response:** Rather than immediately calling "a council of war," Hezekiah first went to God. Henry notes: "When the church's enemies are very daring and threatening it is the wisdom and duty of the church's friends to apply to God, appeal to him, and leave their cause with him."

**On handling blasphemy:** Believers should respond to attacks on God's honor with grief and humiliation. Henry emphasizes that "Royal robes are not too good to be rent, nor royal flesh too good to be clothed with sackcloth, in humiliation for indignities done to God."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2:** Henry highlights Hezekiah's immediate response of rending his clothes and seeking God, contrasting this with others who continued in "joy and gladness" during the crisis (referencing Isaiah 22:12-14).

**Verse 3:** The metaphor of children coming "to the birth" without "strength to bring forth" represents Jerusalem's critical moment - ready for deliverance but powerless to achieve it alone.

**Verses 6-7:** God's brief response through Isaiah demonstrates that "short threatening from the mouth of God would do execution, when all the impotent menaces that came from Rabshakeh's mouth would vanish into air."

**Verse 14:** Henry notes Hezekiah's wisdom in "spreading" Sennacherib's letter "before the Lord" - literally laying out the problem before God rather than merely describing it.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves numerous biblical connections throughout:

- **Psalm 73:17** - Going to God's sanctuary for understanding during distress
- **Joel 2:17** - Priests praying for people in times of trouble  
- **Genesis 20:7** - Prophets as intercessors ("He is a prophet, and he shall pray for thee")
- **Psalm 74:22** - Appealing to God to "plead thy own cause"
- **Isaiah 22:12-14** - Contrasting appropriate and inappropriate responses to crisis
- **2 Chronicles 32:3** - Hezekiah's earlier military preparations

Henry's commentary emphasizes that this historical account demonstrates timeless principles about God's faithfulness, the power of prayer, and proper responses to seemingly impossible circumstances. The chapter serves as both historical record and spiritual instruction for believers facing overwhelming opposition.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings Chapter 20: Summary

Matthew Henry presents this chapter as a study in divine sovereignty over life and death, emphasizing that **"the days of the former [Sennacherib] shortened, of the latter [Hezekiah] prolonged."** The commentary explores how God responds to faithful prayer while maintaining His ultimate authority. The commentary demonstrates Henry's theological depth while maintaining practical relevance, showing how God's sovereignty and human responsibility intersect in prayer, faith, and faithful living.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 21: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this chapter as a stark contrast to Hezekiah's righteous reign, emphasizing **divine judgment upon apostasy**. The central theme is how "when the holy seed degenerate, they are commonly worse than the worst of the profane."

The **desecration of the temple** represents the ultimate affront to God—placing idols "immediately under his eye" and attempting to "turn God out of his own house." This symbolizes complete covenant abandonment.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications:

**Dangers of Early Authority**: Young leaders face particular temptations—being "puffed up with pride" and "easily wrought upon by seducers." However, Henry notes this doesn't excuse sin, citing Josiah who "came to it younger than he and yet acted well."

**Verses 1-3**: Henry emphasizes the tragedy of undoing his father's reforms, calling it trampling "upon the dust, and affront[ing] the memory, of his worthy father."

**Verse 6**: The practice of making "his son pass through the fire" is seen as contempt for circumcision's covenant seal.

**Verses 12-13**: The judgment prophecy using "the line of Samaria, and the plummet of the house of Ahab" means Jerusalem will receive the same measured destruction as Israel's capital and Ahab's dynasty.

**Verse 16**: The shedding of "innocent blood" likely included prophets opposing idolatry, with Henry noting the Jewish tradition that Isaiah was "sawn asunder" under Manasseh.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive scriptural connections throughout:

- **Isaiah 33:17, 20**: Links to Hezekiah as "the king in his beauty" when "Jerusalem was a quiet habitation"
- **Isaiah 1:26**: Describes Jerusalem's former status as "a city of righteousness" 
- **Isaiah 28:17**: Explains the "line" and "plummet" judgment metaphors
- **Hebrews 11:37**: Connects to those "sawn asunder," likely referencing Isaiah's martyrdom
- **Matthew 23:32**: References Jerusalem's measure of "blood-guiltiness" being filled "up to the brim"
- **2 Kings 18:22**: Cites Rabshakeh's criticism of Hezekiah's reforms

Henry's commentary transforms this dark chapter into a sobering meditation on **covenant unfaithfulness, leadership responsibility, and divine justice**, while maintaining hope in God's preserving grace for the faithful remnant.

# Matthew Henry Commentary Summary: 2 Kings Chapter 22

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes the **sovereignty of divine grace** in Josiah's transformation, noting how "the father passed by and left to perish in his sin, the son a chosen vessel." Despite being born to wicked King Amon with no good education or examples, God's grace made Josiah "an eminent saint," demonstrating that "nothing is too hard for that grace to do."

The **providential preservation of Scripture** emerges as a central theme. Henry marvels at God's hand in preserving the book of the law, whether it was "carelessly mislaid," "maliciously concealed," or "carefully laid up by friends." He declares: "God's care of the Bible is a plain indication of his interest in it."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications:

**On Biblical Authority**: He warns against substituting Scripture with "summaries, extracts, or collections" that omit crucial elements like promises and threats. "No summaries...can be effectual to convey and preserve the knowledge of God and his will like the Bible itself."

**On Spiritual Diligence**: Josiah's late start in reform should motivate believers who "have begun late" to "work hard." The consideration of time lost should quicken more vigorous service to God.

**On Divine Blessing for Faithful Work**: "Those that do their duty according to their knowledge shall have their knowledge increased. To him that hath shall be given."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2**: Henry contrasts Solomon's warning about child kings with the blessing of godly young rulers, referencing England's Edward VI as a parallel to Josiah.

**Verses 3-7**: The temple repairs demonstrate both proper stewardship and the trustworthiness of honest workers, though Henry questions whether complete accountability might have been wiser.

**Verses 8-10**: Henry suggests the discovered book was likely the original manuscript from Deuteronomy 31:24, calling its discovery a "token for good" that would direct Josiah's reformation.

**Verses 11-13**: Josiah's heart-rending response reveals he had likely only known abbreviated versions of the law, making the full promises and threats "new to him."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry weaves extensive scriptural connections throughout:
- **Ezekiel 18:14** - on children not following parents' sins
- **Romans 11:24** - on being grafted from wild to good olive
- **2 Chronicles 34:3,8** - for chronological details of Josiah's reforms
- **Deuteronomy 17:18; 31:10-11** - on neglected commands for copying and reading the law
- **Isaiah 65:1** - "found when he sought it not"

Henry concludes by emphasizing that discovering God's word while engaged in faithful temple repair illustrates how "they were about a good work...when they found the book of the law." This discovery would become "an abundant recompence for all their care and cost about the repair of the temple," setting the stage for Josiah's great reformation detailed in the following chapter.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 23: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Josiah's reformation as a model of faithful leadership despite inevitable judgment. The central theme is that **duty must be performed regardless of ultimate outcomes**—Josiah "would do his duty, and then leave the event to God" even after learning Jerusalem's destruction was unavoidable. Henry emphasizes the covenant relationship, showing how the "book of the law was the book of the covenant" whereby God promises to be their God if they are His people.

The commentary reveals the shocking extent of corruption within God's house, demonstrating how "corporal and spiritual whoredom went together." Henry sees this as divine judgment: "Those that dishonoured their God were justly left thus to dishonour themselves" (referencing Romans 1:24).

## Key Practical Applications

**Public Reformation**: Henry advocates for collaborative religious reform, noting that "Parliaments are no diminution at all to the honour and power of good princes, but a great support to them." Leaders should involve representatives to make reformation "a national act."

**Scripture Knowledge**: Henry stresses that "it is really the interest of princes to promote the knowledge of the scriptures in their dominions" and that "all people are concerned to know the scripture, and all in authority to spread the knowledge of it."

**Covenant Commitment**: He emphasizes the value of formal religious commitments: "It is of good use to oblige ourselves to our duty with all possible solemnity... He that bears an honest mind does not shrink from positive engagements: fast bind, fast find."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry highlights Josiah's personal involvement—he "read it himself" rather than delegating, showing leaders shouldn't consider themselves "below" direct ministry. The commentary details the shocking idolatry found even within the temple: horses "given to the sun" at the temple entrance and "houses of the Sodomites" nearby where lewdness was practiced "under pretence of religion."

Henry notes the tragic irony that despite thorough reformation, "within a few years Jerusalem was utterly destroyed, and even this did not save it; for the generality of the people, after all, hated to be reformed."

## Cross-References

Henry connects the text to Jeremiah 6:29-30 regarding Israel as "reprobate silver," Romans 1:24 on divine abandonment to vile affections, Psalm 19:5 concerning God's swiftness, and multiple references linking spiritual and physical corruption. He also references 2 Kings 11:14 regarding the king's pillar position during covenant renewal.

## Matthew Henry's Commentary on 2 Kings 24: Summary

### Main Theological Themes

Henry emphasizes **divine sovereignty over human affairs** throughout this chapter. He notes that while Nebuchadnezzar appears as the dominant force, "The Lord sent against him all these bands" and "at the commandment of the Lord came this upon Judah." Henry stresses that "many are serving God's purposes who are not aware of it," showing how God uses even pagan nations to accomplish His will.

The commentary highlights **the certainty of God's judgment** against sin. Henry observes that God "waited" to see if the nation would repent after Manasseh's sins, but when they remained impenitent, judgment came. He warns that "time will not wear out the guilt of sin and that reprieves are not pardons," emphasizing that "threatenings will be fulfilled as certainly as promises, if the sinner's repentance prevent not."

### Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several sobering lessons for individuals and nations. He warns about **the consequences of unfaithfulness**, noting that if Jehoiakim "had served his God as he should have done, he would not have been servant to the king of Babylon." The commentary emphasizes how rebellion against earthly authority often stems from rebellion against divine authority.

The text also highlights **the importance of national repentance** for past sins. Henry notes that nations must "lament the sins of their fathers, lest they smart for them," as generational sins can bring ongoing consequences even when individual forgiveness has been granted.

### Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Regarding Jehoiachin's brief three-month reign, Henry poignantly observes: "He came to the crown, not to have the honour of wearing it, but the shame of losing it." He describes the young king as one "thrust into a falling house, a sinking throne," inheriting only "his own miseries" from his father's sins.

Henry notes that Jehoiachin "reigned long enough to show that he justly smarted for his fathers' sins" because he continued their evil ways, demonstrating that inherited consequences don't excuse personal responsibility.

### Cross-References and Prophetic Fulfillment

Henry extensively connects the events to prophetic fulfillment, citing Deuteronomy 28:49 regarding foreign invasion and Isaiah 20:17 about Babylon carrying away temple treasures. He references Jeremiah's prophecies about Jehoiakim's ignominious burial "with the burial of an ass" (Jeremiah 22:18-19).

The commentary emphasizes how "the word of God was fulfilled" and that "rather shall heaven and earth pass away, than any word of God fall to the ground," demonstrating Scripture's reliability in both promises and warnings.

Chapter 25
----------------------------------------
# Matthew Henry Commentary on 2 Kings 25: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents the fall of Jerusalem as divine judgment against persistent sin and rebellion. He emphasizes that **"Those that by sin have provoked God to leave them will find that innumerable evils will compass them about."** The destruction represents God's sovereignty in judgment—when Jerusalem lost divine protection, it became vulnerable to enemies.

The chapter demonstrates God's faithfulness to His warnings through the prophets. Henry notes how **"two prophecies that seemed to contradict one another were both fulfilled"** when Zedekiah was brought to Babylon but couldn't see it due to his blinding, reconciling Jeremiah and Ezekiel's seemingly conflicting prophecies.

Henry also emphasizes God's patience, noting that even after capturing the city, the Chaldeans waited a month before final destruction, giving **"space God gave them to repent, after all the foregoing days of his patience, but in vain."**

## Key Practical Applications

**Divine Protection Requires Obedience**: Henry warns that external religious symbols cannot protect those living in sin. The people **"trusted to the temple, as if that would protect them in their sins,"** but found it **"a refuge of lies"** when they had profaned it.

**Consequences of Rebellion**: Zedekiah's attempt to **"shake off his yoke, when he was no way able to do it, nor took the right method by making God his friend first"** illustrates the futility of opposing God's will without seeking His favor.

**Providence's Reversals**: Henry observes how **"Providence sometimes remarkably humbles the proud and favours those of low degree"** when the poor remained in their homeland while the wealthy became prisoners.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-7 (The Siege)**: Henry emphasizes that escape from God's judgment is impossible: **"those as much deceive themselves who think to escape God's judgments as those who think to brave them."**

**Verses 8-17 (Temple Destruction)**: The burning of God's house demonstrates that **"God would show how little cares for the external pomp of his worship when the life and power of religion are neglected."**

**Verses 18-21 (Leadership Executed)**: The destruction of both royal and priestly leadership symbolizes the complete end of the old covenant order.

**Verses 27-30 (Jehoiachin's Release)**: This concluding mercy suggests hope for restoration and God's continuing covenant faithfulness.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this chapter to prophetic fulfillment, citing **Ezekiel 4:16** regarding the famine, **Ezekiel 5:10** on cannibalism, and **Lamentations 4:3** on suffering. He references **Jeremiah 7:4** about false trust in the temple and multiple passages showing how seemingly contradictory prophecies were fulfilled.

He notes the remarkable historical parallel that **"the second temple was burnt by the Romans the same month, and the same day of the month, that the first temple was burnt by the Chaldeans."**

Henry concludes with theological hope, suggesting that since the people forfeited the promises concerning the physical temple, **"those promises must be understood of the gospel-temple, which is God's rest for ever."**
