
================================================================================
BOOK: Joshua
================================================================================

Chapter 0 (Introduction)
Matthew Henry introduces Joshua as part of the historical prophetic books. The book records Israel's history under Joshua's command: their entrance into Canaan (ch. 1-5), conquest of Canaan (ch. 6-12), distribution of the land (ch. 13-21), and establishment of religion (ch. 22-24). Henry emphasizes Joshua as a type of Christ - both names mean "He shall save." Joshua saves God's people from Canaanites; Jesus saves them from sins. Joshua is the captain of salvation who leads God's people into rest, though the ultimate rest comes through Christ.

Chapter 1
God commissions Joshua to lead Israel into Canaan after Moses' death, promising success if he meditates on the law day and night. Joshua is commanded three times to "be strong and courageous." The two and a half tribes (Reuben, Gad, half-Manasseh) confirm their commitment to help conquer Canaan before returning to their inheritance east of Jordan. Henry emphasizes that ministers must be diligent students of Scripture, and that God's promises come with conditions of faithful obedience.

Chapter 2
Joshua sends two spies to Jericho who are sheltered by Rahab the harlot. She hides them from the king's men, confesses faith in Israel's God, and secures a promise of protection for her family. The spies escape by a scarlet cord and report that the land's inhabitants are terrified. Henry sees Rahab as an example of saving faith - a Gentile and former sinner who believed and acted on her faith, becoming an ancestor of Christ.

Chapter 3
Israel crosses the Jordan River miraculously during flood season. The priests carrying the ark step into the water, and the river parts, allowing all Israel to cross on dry ground. Henry draws parallels between this crossing and the Red Sea crossing, noting that God's power is the same whether stopping waters from above (Jordan) or dividing them (Red Sea). The crossing represents the believer's passage from wilderness wandering to promised inheritance.

Chapter 4
Joshua commands twelve stones be set up at Gilgal as a memorial of the Jordan crossing, and twelve stones placed where the priests stood in the riverbed. This monument serves to teach future generations of God's mighty works. Henry emphasizes the importance of religious instruction of children and the value of memorials that prompt questions about God's faithfulness. The crossing exalted Joshua as Moses' worthy successor.

Chapter 5
At Gilgal, Joshua circumcises the new generation born in the wilderness, "rolling away the reproach of Egypt." They celebrate Passover, and the manna ceases as they eat Canaan's produce. The commander of the Lord's army (the pre-incarnate Christ) appears to Joshua with drawn sword, declaring the ground holy. Henry notes this theophany prepares Joshua for the battles ahead and reminds him that God, not human might, wins victories.

Chapter 6
God gives Joshua unusual battle instructions for Jericho: march around the city once daily for six days, then seven times on the seventh day with trumpets blowing. The walls fall miraculously. Israel destroys everything except Rahab and her family, who are saved according to the spies' oath. Joshua pronounces a curse on anyone who rebuilds Jericho. Henry emphasizes that faith obeys God's commands even when they seem foolish to human wisdom.

Chapter 7
Israel's defeat at Ai reveals hidden sin - Achan has stolen devoted things from Jericho. After lots identify the guilty party, Achan confesses taking a Babylonian garment, silver, and gold. He and his family are stoned and burned in the Valley of Achor ("trouble"). Henry warns that one person's sin affects the whole community, that hidden sins will be exposed, and that covetousness leads to greater destruction than any temporal gain could justify.

Chapter 8
After purging Achan's sin, God instructs Joshua to take Ai using an ambush strategy. The city falls, its king is hanged, and Joshua builds an altar on Mount Ebal where he reads all the law before assembled Israel with blessings and curses. Henry notes that military strategy is appropriate alongside trusting God, and emphasizes the importance of renewing covenant commitment immediately after victory.

Chapter 9
The Gibeonites deceive Israel with a ruse - wearing worn clothes and carrying moldy bread, they pretend to be from a distant land. Israel makes a peace treaty without consulting God. When the deception is discovered, Israel keeps the oath but makes the Gibeonites woodcutters and water carriers for the tabernacle. Henry warns against making important decisions without prayer, and notes that even Gentiles could be incorporated into Israel's service through submission.

Chapter 10
Five Amorite kings attack Gibeon for making peace with Israel. Joshua's army marches all night, God sends hailstones that kill more enemies than Israel's swords, and the sun stands still over Gibeon. Joshua captures and executes the five kings in a cave at Makkedah, then conquers the entire southern region. Henry marvels at God's control over creation for his people's sake and sees the hanging of kings as a type of Christ's victory over principalities.

Chapter 11
Joshua turns north to face a massive coalition led by Jabin of Hazor. God commands Joshua to hamstring their horses and burn their chariots - Israel must not trust in military technology. The northern campaign succeeds, and Joshua takes the whole land as God commanded Moses. Henry emphasizes that complete obedience brings complete victory, and that Israel's wars fulfilled God's patient judgment on nations whose iniquity was finally "full."

Chapter 12
This chapter catalogs Israel's conquests - two kings defeated under Moses east of Jordan (Sihon and Og), and thirty-one kings under Joshua west of Jordan. Henry notes this summary shows God's faithfulness to his promises and the extent of the blessing. The detailed list serves as evidence that what God promised, he performed.

Chapter 13
God tells the aging Joshua that much land remains to be conquered, but he should begin dividing it by lot. The chapter recounts the inheritance Moses gave to Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh east of Jordan. Levi receives no territorial inheritance because "the Lord God of Israel is their inheritance." Henry notes that old age should prompt us to finish our work, and that God's ministers are provided for differently than others.

Chapter 14
The land west of Jordan is divided by lot among nine and a half tribes, supervised by Joshua, Eleazar, and tribal heads. Caleb requests and receives Hebron as promised by Moses 45 years earlier, despite the presence of giant Anakim. At 85, Caleb declares himself as strong as at 40 and ready to drive out the giants. Henry praises Caleb's faithfulness over decades and his confidence in God's presence rather than his own strength.

Chapter 15
Judah's inheritance is described - borders, cities, and territories. Caleb conquers Hebron and offers his daughter Achsah to whoever takes Debir; his nephew Othniel succeeds and receives additional land with springs of water at Achsah's request. The Jebusites remain unconquered in Jerusalem. Henry notes that Judah's preeminence fulfilled Jacob's prophecy, and that the failure to drive out Jebusites reflected Israel's spiritual weakness.

Chapter 16
Joseph's descendants (Ephraim and Manasseh) receive the central highlands, fulfilling Jacob's blessing. Ephraim's borders are described, but they fail to drive out Canaanites from Gezer, instead making them tributaries. Henry criticizes this compromise with idolaters and notes that sparing enemies out of covetousness (for tribute) is no better than sparing them out of cowardice.

Chapter 17
The half-tribe of Manasseh west of Jordan receives ten portions - six for Gilead's sons and five for Zelophehad's daughters who claimed their inheritance as God commanded Moses. The tribes of Joseph complain their lot is too small; Joshua challenges them to clear the forested hill country and drive out the Canaanites with their iron chariots. Henry rebukes their ingratitude and laziness.

Chapter 18
The tabernacle is established at Shiloh. Joshua rebukes seven tribes for being slack to possess their inheritance and sends surveyors to divide the remaining land into seven portions. Benjamin's lot falls between Judah and Joseph, including Jericho, Bethel, Gibeon, and part of Jerusalem. Henry emphasizes that spiritual rest and inheritance require active effort to claim what God has promised.

Chapter 19
The remaining six tribal inheritances are assigned: Simeon within Judah's territory (fulfilling Jacob's prophecy of scattering), Zebulun and Issachar in the north (including areas later associated with Christ's ministry), Asher and Naphtali along the coast and northern borders, and Dan in the west (who later migrate north to capture Leshem). Finally, Joshua receives Timnath-serah in Ephraim - served last though greatest, building his own city.

Chapter 20
Six cities of refuge are designated - three west of Jordan (Kedesh, Shechem, Hebron) and three east (Bezer, Ramoth, Golan). These protect those guilty of unintentional manslaughter from the avenger of blood until trial. Henry sees these cities as types of Christ, our refuge from the curse of the law. Even the cities' names are spiritually significant: holy, shoulder (government), fellowship, fortification, exalted, and joy.

Chapter 21
The Levites petition for their cities and receive forty-eight cities with pastureland scattered throughout all tribes - thirteen for priests (Aaron's descendants) and thirty-five for other Levites. The chapter concludes triumphantly: God gave Israel all the promised land, rest from enemies, and not one promise failed. Henry emphasizes that Levites dispersed throughout Israel served as teachers and examples, maintaining religion in every region.

Chapter 22
Joshua dismisses the two and a half eastern tribes with blessing and spoils, charging them to love and serve God. Upon reaching Jordan, they build a large altar, alarming the western tribes who prepare for war against suspected idolatry. Phinehas and ten princes investigate; the eastern tribes explain the altar is not for sacrifice but as a witness of their covenant connection to the Lord's tabernacle. Satisfied, the congregation blesses God. Henry praises both the zeal against idolatry and the wisdom of inquiry before judgment.

Chapter 23
In his old age, Joshua summons Israel's leaders for a farewell charge. He recounts God's faithfulness in giving them victory and rest, warns against intermarriage and idolatry with remaining Canaanites, and solemnly threatens that apostasy will bring God's judgment - the same God who fulfilled every good promise will fulfill every threatened curse. Henry emphasizes Joshua's pastoral concern to secure Israel's perseverance in faith beyond his death.

Chapter 24
At Shechem, Joshua delivers his final address, recounting God's works from Abraham through the conquest. He challenges Israel: "Choose this day whom you will serve... but as for me and my house, we will serve the Lord." The people affirm their choice three times; Joshua makes a covenant, writes it in the law book, and sets up a witness stone. Joshua dies at 110, Eleazar also dies, and Joseph's bones are finally buried at Shechem. Henry notes that Israel served God all Joshua's days - a tribute to godly leadership, but also a warning that such faithfulness rarely outlasts one generation.
