JUDGES - MATTHEW HENRY COMMENTARY SUMMARY
==========================================

INTRODUCTION (Chapter 0)
-------------------------
The book of Judges covers approximately 299 years of Israel's history, from the death of Joshua to the rise of Samuel. It documents the period when "there was no king in Israel" and "every man did that which was right in his own eyes." The book records the repeated cycle of apostasy, oppression, repentance, and deliverance that characterized this era. Four of the judges—Gideon, Barak, Samson, and Jephthah—are canonized among the Old Testament worthies in Hebrews 11:32. The judges were raised up as occasional deliverers rather than hereditary rulers, serving as types of the great Deliverer, our Lord Jesus Christ. Henry notes this period demonstrates both God's faithfulness to His covenant promises and Israel's unfaithfulness to theirs.

CHAPTER 1 - Post-Joshua Conquests and Failures
----------------------------------------------
After Joshua's death, Judah is divinely appointed to lead the continued conquest of Canaan. Judah partners with Simeon, and together they defeat Adoni-bezek at Bezek, cutting off his thumbs and great toes—the same punishment he had inflicted on seventy kings. Jerusalem is taken and burned. Caleb offers his daughter Achsah to whoever conquers Kirjath-sepher; Othniel succeeds and receives both the bride and additional land with springs of water. The chapter then catalogs the failures of various tribes to fully dispossess the Canaanites: Judah could not drive out the inhabitants of the valley because of their iron chariots; Benjamin failed to expel the Jebusites from Jerusalem; Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Asher, and Naphtali all allowed Canaanites to dwell among them, often reducing them to tribute rather than expelling them. Dan was pushed into the mountains. These compromises would prove spiritually fatal, as the remaining Canaanites became snares to Israel.

CHAPTER 2 - The Angel's Rebuke and the Cycle of Sin
---------------------------------------------------
An angel of the Lord comes from Gilgal to Bochim (meaning "weepers") to rebuke Israel for making leagues with the Canaanites and failing to throw down their altars. God declares He will no longer drive out the nations before them; instead, they shall be thorns in Israel's sides and their gods shall be snares. The people weep and sacrifice, but the damage is done. The chapter reviews Joshua's death and burial, and the faithfulness of the generation that served the Lord all the days of the elders who outlived Joshua. But after that generation died, a new generation arose "which knew not the Lord, nor yet the works which he had done for Israel." This began the recurring cycle: Israel forsook the Lord and served Baal and Ashtaroth; God's anger was kindled and He delivered them to spoilers; they were greatly distressed; God raised up judges who delivered them; but after the judge died, they returned to corruption worse than before. God allowed nations to remain to test Israel and teach successive generations warfare.

CHAPTER 3 - Othniel, Ehud, and Shamgar
--------------------------------------
The chapter identifies the nations left to prove Israel: the five lords of the Philistines, the Canaanites, Sidonians, and Hivites. Israel intermarried with these peoples and served their gods. The first oppression came from Cushan-rishathaim king of Mesopotamia for eight years. God raised up Othniel, Caleb's nephew and the first judge, who was empowered by the Spirit of the Lord, went to war, and delivered Israel. The land had rest forty years until Othniel died.

Israel again did evil, and God strengthened Eglon king of Moab against them for eighteen years. The Israelites cried to the Lord, who raised up Ehud, a left-handed Benjamite. Ehud made a two-edged dagger, concealed it on his right thigh, and brought tribute to Eglon. After dismissing his attendants, Ehud returned privately to Eglon, claiming a secret message from God. When the obese king rose, Ehud thrust the dagger into his belly so that even the handle went in after the blade and the fat closed over it. Ehud escaped through the porch, locked the doors, and fled while the servants delayed, thinking their master was in his summer parlor. Ehud rallied Israel, seized the fords of Jordan, slew about 10,000 Moabites, and secured eighty years of rest.

Shamgar son of Anath is briefly mentioned as slaying 600 Philistines with an ox goad, also delivering Israel.

CHAPTER 4 - Deborah, Barak, and Jael
------------------------------------
After Ehud's death, Israel again sinned and was sold into the hand of Jabin king of Canaan, who reigned in Hazor. His captain Sisera, commanding 900 iron chariots, mightily oppressed Israel for twenty years. Deborah, a prophetess and the only female judge, held court under a palm tree between Ramah and Bethel. She summoned Barak of Kedesh-naphtali and delivered God's command to gather 10,000 men from Naphtali and Zebulun at Mount Tabor. God promised to draw Sisera to the river Kishon and deliver him into Barak's hand. Barak agreed to go only if Deborah accompanied him; she consented but prophesied that the honor of killing Sisera would go to a woman.

Barak assembled his forces at Tabor. Sisera gathered his chariots at the river Kishon. The Lord discomfited Sisera's army; Barak pursued and destroyed them all—not a man was left. Sisera fled on foot to the tent of Jael, wife of Heber the Kenite, whose family had peace with Jabin. Jael welcomed him, gave him milk, and covered him. When he slept from exhaustion, she took a tent peg and hammer and drove the peg through his temples into the ground, killing him. When Barak arrived pursuing Sisera, Jael showed him the dead captain. That day God subdued Jabin, and Israel continued pressing until Jabin was destroyed.

CHAPTER 5 - The Song of Deborah
-------------------------------
This magnificent victory hymn, sung by Deborah and Barak, celebrates God's deliverance. It praises those who willingly offered themselves for battle and calls for the rehearsal of God's righteous acts. The song poetically describes the Lord's march from Seir, when the earth trembled and heavens dropped water. It contrasts the desolate conditions under oppression—when highways were unused and villages abandoned—with the awakening when Deborah arose as a mother in Israel.

The song praises the tribes that participated: the princes of Issachar were with Deborah, and Barak was sent on foot into the valley. But it pronounces curses on Meroz for not coming to help the Lord against the mighty, and rebukes Reuben for staying among the sheepfolds, Dan for remaining in ships, and Asher for abiding on the seashore. The most vivid stanza describes Jael's killing of Sisera: "She put her hand to the nail, and her right hand to the workmen's hammer; and with the hammer she smote Sisera, she smote off his head." The song ironically depicts Sisera's mother waiting at the window, wondering why his chariot delays, while her ladies answer that he must be dividing the spoil. The song concludes: "So let all thine enemies perish, O Lord: but let them that love him be as the sun when he goeth forth in his might." The land had rest forty years.

CHAPTER 6 - The Calling of Gideon
---------------------------------
Israel's renewed apostasy brought seven years of severe Midianite oppression. The Midianites, Amalekites, and eastern peoples invaded like locusts, destroying crops and livestock, driving Israelites to hide in mountain dens and caves. Israel was greatly impoverished. When they cried to the Lord, He first sent a prophet to remind them of their deliverance from Egypt and their disobedience in serving the gods of the Amorites.

The angel of the Lord appeared to Gideon as he threshed wheat secretly in the winepress to hide it from Midianites. The angel greeted him: "The Lord is with thee, thou mighty man of valour." Gideon questioned why, if God was with them, all these troubles had befallen them and where were the miracles their fathers told of. The Lord commissioned him to save Israel, promising, "Surely I will be with thee." Gideon protested his family was poor in Manasseh and he was least in his father's house. He requested a sign and prepared an offering of a kid and unleavened cakes. The angel touched them with his staff, fire rose from the rock consuming the offering, and the angel vanished. Gideon feared death from seeing God but was assured, "Peace be unto thee; fear not: thou shalt not die." He built an altar called Jehovah-shalom.

That night God commanded Gideon to destroy his father's altar to Baal and the grove beside it, then build an altar to the Lord and offer his father's bullock. Gideon obeyed by night, fearing his family and the townsmen. When discovered, the men demanded his death, but his father Joash defended him: "If Baal be a god, let him plead for himself." Gideon was renamed Jerubbaal ("let Baal plead").

When the Midianites and allies gathered in the valley of Jezreel, the Spirit of the Lord clothed Gideon. He blew a trumpet, gathering Abiezer, and sent messengers throughout Manasseh, Asher, Zebulun, and Naphtali. Still uncertain, Gideon requested the famous fleece signs: first, let the fleece be wet and ground dry; then let the fleece be dry and ground wet. God graciously granted both confirmations.

CHAPTER 7 - Gideon's Victory with Three Hundred
------------------------------------------------
At God's command, Gideon reduced his army to prevent Israel from boasting of self-deliverance. First, 22,000 fearful men were sent home, leaving 10,000. Still too many—God directed a water-drinking test at the spring of Harod. Those who lapped water from their hands, remaining vigilant, were kept; those who knelt to drink were dismissed. Only 300 remained. God promised: "By the three hundred men that lapped will I save you."

That night God invited Gideon to spy on the Midianite camp with his servant Phurah. He overheard a soldier recounting a dream: a barley cake tumbled into the camp and overturned a tent. His companion interpreted it as "the sword of Gideon... into his hand hath God delivered Midian." Encouraged, Gideon worshipped and returned to rally his men: "Arise; for the Lord hath delivered into your hand the host of Midian."

He divided the 300 into three companies, giving each man a trumpet, an empty pitcher, and a torch inside the pitcher. They surrounded the Midianite camp in three watches of the night. At Gideon's signal, they blew trumpets, broke pitchers, held torches aloft, and shouted, "The sword of the Lord, and of Gideon!" The Lord set every man's sword against his fellow throughout the vast host. The Midianites fled toward Beth-shittah and the border of Abel-meholah. Gideon called out Naphtali, Asher, and all Manasseh to pursue. He sent messengers to Ephraim to seize the fords of Jordan and the waters of Beth-barah. Ephraim captured and beheaded two Midianite princes, Oreb and Zeeb, bringing their heads to Gideon.

CHAPTER 8 - Gideon's Later Years
--------------------------------
The Ephraimites angrily confronted Gideon for not summoning them initially. He diplomatically defused their anger: "Is not the gleaning of the grapes of Ephraim better than the vintage of Abiezer?"—their capture of Oreb and Zeeb exceeded his initial victories. His soft answer turned away wrath.

Gideon pursued the fleeing kings Zebah and Zalmunna with his weary 300 across Jordan. He requested bread from Succoth and Penuel but was refused—they mocked that he hadn't yet captured the Midianite kings. Gideon promised to tear their flesh with thorns and briers and break down Penuel's tower. He overtook the 15,000 remaining Midianites at Karkor, routing them and capturing both kings. Returning, he punished Succoth's elders with desert thorns and briers and demolished Penuel's tower, killing its men.

Gideon asked Zebah and Zalmunna about men they killed at Tabor; they described them as resembling king's sons. Gideon revealed they were his brothers: "As the Lord liveth, if ye had saved them alive, I would not slay you." He commanded his firstborn Jether to execute them, but the youth was afraid. The kings taunted, "Rise thou, and fall upon us: for as the man is, so is his strength." Gideon slew them himself and took their camels' ornaments.

Israel offered Gideon hereditary kingship: "Rule thou over us." He refused: "I will not rule over you, neither shall my son rule over you: the Lord shall rule over you." However, he requested the golden earrings from the spoil—1,700 shekels of gold, plus ornaments, chains, and purple raiment. He made an ephod and set it in his city Ophrah. "All Israel went thither a whoring after it: which thing became a snare unto Gideon, and to his house."

Midian was subdued and lifted not up their head again. The land had rest forty years in Gideon's days. He had seventy sons from many wives, plus Abimelech by a concubine in Shechem. Gideon died at a good old age and was buried in Ophrah. Immediately Israel turned again to Baalim and made Baal-berith their god, forgetting the Lord and showing no kindness to Gideon's house.

CHAPTER 9 - Abimelech's Tyranny and Death
-----------------------------------------
Abimelech, Gideon's son by a Shechemite concubine, conspired to seize power. He persuaded his mother's kinsmen in Shechem to support his sole rule rather than governance by all seventy of Gideon's sons. They gave him seventy pieces of silver from the temple of Baal-berith. With this he hired worthless, reckless fellows and went to Ophrah, where he murdered his seventy brothers upon one stone. Only Jotham, the youngest, escaped by hiding.

The Shechemites made Abimelech king by the oak of the pillar. Jotham ascended Mount Gerizim and cried a famous parable to them: The trees sought a king. They asked the olive tree, which refused to leave its fatness; the fig tree, which refused to leave its sweetness; the vine, which refused to leave its wine that cheers God and man. Finally the bramble accepted, inviting the trees to trust its shadow—but warning that if not in truth, fire would come from the bramble to devour the cedars of Lebanon. Jotham applied this: if they had dealt truly with Jerubbaal's house, let them rejoice in Abimelech and he in them; if not, let fire come from Abimelech to devour Shechem and from Shechem to devour Abimelech. Jotham fled to Beer.

After three years, God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the Shechemites, who dealt treacherously with him. This was God's judgment for the violence against Gideon's seventy sons. The Shechemites set ambushes against Abimelech. Gaal son of Ebed came to Shechem and roused the people against Abimelech. Zebul, the city ruler loyal to Abimelech, secretly warned him. At dawn Abimelech attacked; Zebul drove out Gaal and his company.

The next day Abimelech destroyed Shechem, slew the people, beat down the city, and sowed it with salt. When Shechem's tower-dwellers took refuge in the stronghold of Baal-berith's temple, Abimelech set fire to it, killing about a thousand men and women. He then attacked Thebez and besieged its strong tower. As he approached to burn its door, a woman cast a piece of millstone upon his head, cracking his skull. Dying, he commanded his armor-bearer: "Draw thy sword, and slay me, that men say not of me, A woman slew him." So Abimelech died. Thus God requited the wickedness of Abimelech and the men of Shechem, and upon them came the curse of Jotham.

CHAPTER 10 - Tola, Jair, and Israel's Repentance
------------------------------------------------
After Abimelech, Tola son of Puah, a man of Issachar dwelling in Shamir in Mount Ephraim, arose to defend Israel. He judged Israel twenty-three years, then died and was buried in Shamir.

Jair the Gileadite succeeded him and judged Israel twenty-two years. He had thirty sons who rode on thirty donkey colts and had thirty cities in Gilead called Havoth-jair. He died and was buried in Camon.

Israel again did evil, serving the Baals, Ashtaroth, and the gods of Syria, Sidon, Moab, Ammon, and the Philistines, forsaking the Lord entirely. God's anger was kindled, and He sold them into the hands of the Philistines and Ammonites. For eighteen years they oppressed Israel beyond Jordan in Gilead; the Ammonites also crossed Jordan to fight Judah, Benjamin, and Ephraim, so that Israel was greatly distressed.

Israel cried to the Lord, confessing: "We have sinned against thee, both because we have forsaken our God, and also served Baalim." The Lord rebuked them, recounting His past deliverances from Egypt, the Amorites, Ammonites, Philistines, Sidonians, Amalekites, and Maonites. "Yet ye have forsaken me, and served other gods: wherefore I will deliver you no more. Go and cry unto the gods which ye have chosen; let them deliver you." Israel persisted in confession: "We have sinned: do thou unto us whatsoever seemeth good unto thee; deliver us only, we pray thee, this day." They put away the foreign gods and served the Lord. "His soul was grieved for the misery of Israel." When Ammon encamped in Gilead and Israel gathered at Mizpeh, they sought a leader to fight Ammon.

CHAPTER 11 - Jephthah's Vow and Victory
---------------------------------------
Jephthah the Gileadite was a mighty man of valor, but his mother was a harlot. His half-brothers by Gilead's wife expelled him: "Thou shalt not inherit in our father's house; for thou art the son of a strange woman." He fled to the land of Tob, where vain men gathered to him and went out raiding.

When Ammon made war, Gilead's elders sought Jephthah, asking him to be their captain. He reminded them: "Did not ye hate me, and expel me?" They promised that if he fought Ammon, he would be their head. Jephthah agreed, and they made covenant before the Lord at Mizpeh.

Jephthah first attempted diplomacy, sending messengers to the king of Ammon asking why he had come to fight. The king claimed Israel had taken his land from the Arnon to the Jabbok and Jordan when they came from Egypt. Jephthah responded with detailed history: Israel did not take Moab or Ammon's land but was refused passage; they fought Sihon king of the Amorites, who attacked them, and took his territory, which the Lord gave them. "Wilt not thou possess that which Chemosh thy god giveth thee to possess? So whomsoever the Lord our God shall drive out from before us, them will we possess." Israel had dwelt there 300 years without Moab reclaiming it—why now? Jephthah concluded: "The Lord the Judge be judge this day between the children of Israel and the children of Ammon." The Ammonite king ignored him.

The Spirit of the Lord came upon Jephthah. He passed through Gilead and Manasseh to Mizpeh. He vowed: "If thou shalt without fail deliver the children of Ammon into mine hands, then it shall be, that whatsoever cometh forth of the doors of my house to meet me, when I return in peace... shall surely be the Lord's, and I will offer it up for a burnt offering."

Jephthah passed over to fight Ammon and smote them from Aroer to Minnith with very great slaughter—twenty cities. Ammon was subdued.

When Jephthah returned home, his daughter—his only child—came out to meet him with timbrels and dancing. Seeing her, he tore his clothes: "Alas, my daughter! thou hast brought me very low... I have opened my mouth unto the Lord, and I cannot go back." She responded nobly: "My father, if thou hast opened thy mouth unto the Lord, do to me according to that which hath proceeded out of thy mouth." She asked only for two months to bewail her virginity upon the mountains with her companions. He granted it, and after two months she returned, and he did to her according to his vow. She had known no man. It became a custom in Israel for daughters to lament Jephthah's daughter four days yearly.

CHAPTER 12 - Conflict with Ephraim and Minor Judges
----------------------------------------------------
The Ephraimites gathered and crossed to Zaphon, confronting Jephthah angrily: "Wherefore passedst thou over to fight against the children of Ammon, and didst not call us to go with thee? we will burn thine house upon thee with fire." Jephthah replied that he had called them, but they did not deliver him from Ammon; he took his life in his hands and the Lord gave him victory. "Wherefore then are ye come up unto me this day, to fight against me?"

Jephthah gathered Gilead's men and fought Ephraim, who had insulted them: "Ye Gileadites are fugitives of Ephraim among the Ephraimites, and among the Manassites." Gilead took the fords of Jordan. When Ephraimite fugitives sought to cross, saying "Let me go over," they were tested: "Say now Shibboleth." The Ephraimites' dialect made them say "Sibboleth," unable to pronounce it correctly. By this test, 42,000 Ephraimites were identified and slain. Jephthah judged Israel six years, died, and was buried in Gilead.

After him, Ibzan of Bethlehem judged Israel. He had thirty sons and thirty daughters; he sent out his daughters and brought in thirty daughters from abroad for his sons. He judged seven years, died, and was buried in Bethlehem.

Elon the Zebulonite succeeded him, judging Israel ten years before dying and being buried in Aijalon in Zebulun.

Abdon son of Hillel the Pirathonite judged Israel, having forty sons and thirty grandsons who rode on seventy donkey colts. He judged eight years, died, and was buried in Pirathon in Ephraim in the Amalekites' hill country.

CHAPTER 13 - The Birth of Samson
--------------------------------
Israel again did evil, and the Lord delivered them to the Philistines for forty years. There was a man of Zorah, of the family of the Danites, named Manoah, whose wife was barren. The angel of the Lord appeared to her, announcing she would conceive and bear a son. He commanded her to drink no wine or strong drink and eat nothing unclean, for her son would be a Nazarite to God from the womb, and "no razor shall come on his head." He would begin to deliver Israel from the Philistines.

She told her husband about the "man of God" whose countenance was "like the countenance of an angel of God, very terrible." She hadn't asked his name or origin. Manoah prayed that the man of God would return to teach them how to raise the child. God heard, and the angel appeared again to the woman in the field. She ran to fetch Manoah, who asked, "Art thou the man that spakest unto the woman?" Receiving confirmation, he asked what the child's manner of life should be. The angel repeated the instructions about the mother's abstinence from wine and unclean food.

Manoah offered to prepare a kid for the visitor. The angel declined eating but suggested offering it as a burnt offering to the Lord. When Manoah asked his name, the angel replied, "Why askest thou thus after my name, seeing it is secret [wonderful]?" Manoah offered the kid and a meat offering upon a rock. When the flame rose, the angel of the Lord ascended in the flame, and they fell on their faces. The angel came no more.

Manoah feared: "We shall surely die, because we have seen God." His wife reasoned that if the Lord meant to kill them, He would not have accepted their offering or shown them such things or told them of a son to come. She bore a son and named him Samson. He grew, the Lord blessed him, and the Spirit of the Lord began to move him at times in Mahaneh-dan.

CHAPTER 14 - Samson's Riddle and Marriage
-----------------------------------------
Samson went to Timnath and saw a Philistine woman who pleased him. He told his parents, "Get her for me to wife." They objected: "Is there never a woman among the daughters of thy brethren, or among all my people, that thou goest to take a wife of the uncircumcised Philistines?" But Samson insisted. His parents did not know this was "of the Lord, that he sought an occasion against the Philistines," who then had dominion over Israel.

As Samson and his parents traveled to Timnath, a young lion roared against him. The Spirit of the Lord came mightily upon him, and he tore the lion apart with his bare hands as one would tear a kid. He told no one. He spoke with the woman and she pleased him.

Returning later, he turned aside to see the lion's carcass and found a swarm of bees and honey in it. He scraped honey into his hands, eating as he went, and gave some to his parents, not telling them its source.

At the wedding feast, the custom was a seven-day celebration. Thirty Philistine companions were brought to attend him. Samson proposed a riddle: "Out of the eater came forth meat, and out of the strong came forth sweetness." If they solved it within seven days, he would give them thirty linen garments and thirty changes of raiment; if not, they would give him the same. They agreed.

After three days they couldn't solve it. On the seventh day they threatened Samson's wife: "Entice thy husband, that he may declare unto us the riddle, lest we burn thee and thy father's house with fire." She wept before Samson, accusing him of hating her. She pressed him persistently until on the seventh day he told her, and she told her countrymen. Before sunset they answered: "What is sweeter than honey? and what is stronger than a lion?" Samson recognized the betrayal: "If ye had not plowed with my heifer, ye had not found out my riddle."

The Spirit of the Lord came upon him. He went to Ashkelon, slew thirty men, took their garments, and paid those who expounded the riddle. He went to his father's house in anger. His wife was given to his companion who had been his best man.

CHAPTER 15 - Samson's Vengeance with Firebrands and Jawbone
-----------------------------------------------------------
Later, at wheat harvest, Samson visited his wife with a kid as a gift, intending to go into her chamber. Her father refused entry, explaining he thought Samson utterly hated her, so he gave her to Samson's companion. He offered the younger sister instead. Samson declared himself blameless for what he would do to the Philistines.

He caught 300 foxes, tied them tail to tail in pairs, put a firebrand between each pair, and released them into the Philistines' standing grain, burning shocks, standing corn, vineyards, and olives. When the Philistines discovered it was Samson's revenge for his wife being given away, they burned her and her father with fire. Samson vowed further vengeance, smiting them "hip and thigh with a great slaughter." He then dwelt in the cleft of the rock Etam.

The Philistines invaded Judah and spread at Lehi. When Judah asked why, they answered: "To bind Samson are we come up, to do to him as he hath done to us." Three thousand men of Judah went to Etam's rock and questioned Samson: "Knowest thou not that the Philistines are rulers over us? what is this that thou hast done unto us?" He answered, "As they did unto me, so have I done unto them." They said they had come to bind him and deliver him to the Philistines. He made them swear not to kill him themselves. They bound him with two new ropes and brought him up from the rock.

When he came to Lehi, the Philistines shouted as they met him. The Spirit of the Lord came mightily upon him; the ropes became as flax burned with fire and fell from his hands. He found a fresh jawbone of a donkey, took it, and slew a thousand men with it. He celebrated in verse: "With the jawbone of an ass, heaps upon heaps, with the jaw of an ass have I slain a thousand men." He named the place Ramath-lehi (jawbone hill).

Greatly thirsting, he cried to the Lord: "Thou hast given this great deliverance into the hand of thy servant: and now shall I die for thirst, and fall into the hand of the uncircumcised?" God split a hollow place in Lehi, and water came out. He drank, his spirit revived, and he named the spring En-hakkore (spring of him that called). He judged Israel in the days of the Philistines twenty years.

CHAPTER 16 - Samson's Fall and Death
------------------------------------
Samson went to Gaza and saw a harlot there. The Gazites learned of his presence and lay in wait at the gate all night, planning to kill him at dawn. At midnight Samson arose, took hold of the city gate doors with their two posts and bar, put them on his shoulders, and carried them to the top of a hill before Hebron.

He then loved a woman in the valley of Sorek named Delilah. The lords of the Philistines bribed her with 1,100 pieces of silver each to discover the source of his great strength so they could bind and afflict him. Three times she asked and he gave false answers: bind me with seven fresh bowstrings (he broke them like burned thread); bind me with new ropes (he broke them like thread); weave my seven locks into a web (he pulled away the pin and web). Each time men lay in wait in her chamber.

Delilah pressed him daily with her words and urged him until his soul was vexed unto death. He finally told the truth: "There hath not come a razor upon mine head; for I have been a Nazarite unto God from my mother's womb: if I be shaven, then my strength will go from me, and I shall become weak, and be like any other man."

Delilah perceived he had told all his heart. She called for the Philistine lords, who came with money. She made him sleep upon her knees and called a man to shave his seven locks. His strength left him. She cried, "The Philistines be upon thee, Samson!" He awoke thinking to shake himself free as before, "but he wist not that the Lord was departed from him."

The Philistines seized him, gouged out his eyes, brought him to Gaza, and bound him with bronze fetters. He ground grain in the prison house. But his hair began to grow again.

The Philistine lords gathered to offer a great sacrifice to Dagon their god, celebrating: "Our god hath delivered Samson our enemy into our hand." When their hearts were merry, they called for Samson to entertain them. A lad led the blind Samson between the pillars. About 3,000 men and women were on the roof watching.

Samson asked the lad to position him where he could feel the pillars supporting the temple. He prayed: "O Lord God, remember me, I pray thee, and strengthen me, I pray thee, only this once, O God, that I may be at once avenged of the Philistines for my two eyes." He grasped the two middle pillars, bracing himself, and cried, "Let me die with the Philistines!" He bowed with all his might; the house fell upon the lords and all the people. "So the dead which he slew at his death were more than they which he slew in his life."

His brothers and father's house came, recovered his body, and buried him in the family tomb between Zorah and Eshtaol. He had judged Israel twenty years.

CHAPTER 17 - Micah's Idolatry
-----------------------------
A man of Mount Ephraim named Micah confessed to his mother that he had taken her 1,100 pieces of silver, about which she had cursed. She blessed him and dedicated the silver to the Lord for her son to make a graven and molten image. She gave 200 pieces to a founder, who made the images that were placed in Micah's house. Micah had a house of gods (a shrine), made an ephod and teraphim, and consecrated one of his own sons as priest. "In those days there was no king in Israel, but every man did that which was right in his own eyes."

A young Levite from Bethlehem-judah, of the family of Judah, was sojourning there. He left to find another place and came to Micah's house in Mount Ephraim. Micah asked about his background and invited him to stay: "Dwell with me, and be unto me a father and a priest, and I will give thee ten shekels of silver by the year, and a suit of apparel, and thy victuals." The Levite agreed and became like one of Micah's sons. Micah ordained him as his priest and said, "Now know I that the Lord will do me good, seeing I have a Levite to my priest." He superstitiously believed having a Levite priest guaranteed God's blessing, despite the entire arrangement violating God's law regarding worship and priesthood.

CHAPTER 18 - The Danites' Migration and Idolatry
------------------------------------------------
"In those days there was no king in Israel." The tribe of Dan had not yet received sufficient inheritance and sent five men from Zorah and Eshtaol to spy out land. Coming to Mount Ephraim, they lodged at Micah's house and recognized the young Levite's voice (perhaps his accent or dialect). They asked how he came there. He explained Micah's arrangement. They asked him to inquire of God whether their journey would prosper. The priest answered, "Go in peace: before the Lord is your way wherein ye go."

The five spied out Laish, finding a quiet and secure people, living carelessly after the Sidonian manner, far from Sidon, with no protector. They returned, urging their brethren to attack: "The land is very good... a place where there is no want of any thing that is in the earth."

Six hundred Danites armed for war set out from Zorah and Eshtaol, camping at Kirjath-jearim (hence called Mahaneh-dan). They came to Micah's house. The five spies informed their brethren about the ephod, teraphim, graven and molten images. The six hundred stood at the gate while the five entered and took the sacred objects. The priest protested. They said, "Hold thy peace, lay thine hand upon thy mouth, and go with us, and be to us a father and a priest: is it better for thee to be a priest unto the house of one man, or that thou be a priest unto a tribe and a family in Israel?" The priest's heart was glad; he took the ephod, teraphim, and graven image and went with them.

Micah and his neighbors pursued but, seeing the Danites too strong, returned home grieving: "Ye have taken away my gods which I made, and the priest, and ye are gone away: and what have I more?" The Danites warned him not to let his voice be heard lest angry fellows fall upon him and his household.

The Danites attacked Laish, a quiet and unsuspecting people, smiting them with the sword and burning the city. There was no deliverer, for it was far from Sidon and they had no alliance with anyone. They rebuilt the city and called it Dan after their ancestor. They set up Micah's graven image. Jonathan, son of Gershom, son of Moses (some manuscripts say "Manasseh" to avoid shaming Moses), and his sons became priests to the tribe of Dan until the day of captivity. They continued Micah's graven image all the time the house of God was in Shiloh.

CHAPTER 19 - The Levite's Concubine and the Crime at Gibeah
-----------------------------------------------------------
"In those days, when there was no king in Israel," a Levite sojourning in Mount Ephraim took a concubine from Bethlehem-judah. She played the harlot against him and went to her father's house, staying four months. The Levite went with his servant and donkeys to speak kindly and bring her back. Her father gladly received him and detained him, urging him to stay and enjoy himself. For three days, then four, and into the fifth day, the father persuaded him to eat and drink.

On the fifth day, despite the father's urging to stay, the Levite departed in late afternoon with his concubine and servant. They passed by Jebus (Jerusalem), the Levite refusing to enter a city of strangers not of Israel. They pressed on to Gibeah, which belongs to Benjamin. Entering the city, they sat in the street, for no one offered lodging.

An old man from Mount Ephraim, sojourning in Gibeah, returned from field work at evening. Seeing the travelers, he asked their business. The Levite explained they were going to his home in Mount Ephraim, came from Bethlehem-judah, and were going to the house of the Lord. They had provision but no one received them. The old man welcomed them: "Peace be with thee; howsoever let all thy wants lie upon me; only lodge not in the street." He brought them in, fed the donkeys, and they washed and ate.

As they made merry, wicked men of the city, sons of Belial, surrounded the house, beating on the door, demanding: "Bring forth the man that came into thine house, that we may know him." The old man went out, pleading: "Nay, my brethren, nay, I pray you, do not so wickedly; seeing that this man is come into mine house, do not this folly." He shamefully offered his virgin daughter and the Levite's concubine instead: "Humble ye them, and do with them what seemeth good unto you: but unto this man do not so vile a thing."

The men would not listen. The Levite took his concubine and brought her out to them. They abused her all night until morning and let her go at dawn's breaking. She fell at the door of the house where her master was until daylight. When the Levite rose to depart and opened the door, his concubine lay at the threshold with her hands upon the sill. He said, "Up, and let us be going." There was no answer. She was dead.

He put her upon the donkey and went home. Taking a knife, he divided her body into twelve pieces and sent them throughout all the coasts of Israel. All who saw it said, "There was no such deed done nor seen from the day that the children of Israel came up out of the land of Egypt unto this day: consider of it, take advice, and speak your minds."

CHAPTER 20 - The War Against Benjamin
-------------------------------------
All Israel assembled as one man before the Lord at Mizpeh—from Dan to Beer-sheba and the land of Gilead—400,000 footmen who drew sword. The Benjamites heard that Israel had come to Mizpeh. The Levite, husband of the murdered woman, testified: he came to Gibeah to lodge; the men of Gibeah rose against him, surrounded the house intending to kill him, and forced his concubine until she died. He divided her body and sent it through Israel because they had committed lewdness and folly.

All the people rose as one man, vowing none would return home until they dealt with Gibeah. They cast lots and chose a tenth of the men for provisions while the army went against Gibeah for this vileness in Israel. The tribes sent men through Benjamin demanding surrender of the wicked men of Gibeah to put them to death and purge evil from Israel. But Benjamin refused to listen, instead gathering 26,000 swordsmen plus 700 chosen left-handed Benjamites who could sling stones at a hair and not miss. Israel numbered 400,000 swordsmen.

Israel went up to Bethel and asked God who should go first against Benjamin. The Lord said, "Judah shall go up first." They arrayed for battle at Gibeah, but Benjamin came out and destroyed 22,000 Israelites that day. Israel encouraged themselves and arrayed again. They wept before the Lord until evening, then asked: "Shall I go up again to battle against the children of Benjamin my brother?" The Lord said, "Go up against him." On the second day, Benjamin slew 18,000 more Israelites.

All Israel went to Bethel, wept, sat before the Lord, fasted until evening, and offered burnt offerings and peace offerings. The ark of the covenant was there; Phinehas son of Eleazar son of Aaron stood before it. They asked: "Shall I yet again go out to battle against the children of Benjamin my brother, or shall I cease?" The Lord answered, "Go up; for to morrow I will deliver them into thine hand."

Israel set ambushes around Gibeah. On the third day, they drew Benjamin out from the city by feigning flight as before. Benjamin struck down about thirty Israelites. As they said, "They are smitten down before us, as at the first," the Israelite main force turned. The ambush rushed into Gibeah, smote it with the sword, and set it on fire. A pillar of smoke rose from the city—the signal for the main force to turn. Benjamin looked back and saw the whole city ablaze.

When Israel turned, the Benjamites were terrified, realizing disaster had come. They fled toward the wilderness, but the battle overtook them. Those from the cities Israel destroyed among them. They surrounded Benjamin, pursued and trod them down from Menuhah to east of Gibeah. Eighteen thousand Benjamites fell, all valiant men. Five thousand were struck down on the highways; they pursued to Gidom and slew two thousand more. Twenty-five thousand Benjamite swordsmen fell that day. Six hundred fled to the rock of Rimmon in the wilderness and stayed there four months. Israel turned back on Benjamin's cities, striking them with the sword—men, beasts, everything found—and burned every city.

CHAPTER 21 - Wives for Benjamin
-------------------------------
Israel had sworn at Mizpeh: "There shall not any of us give his daughter unto Benjamin to wife." Now at Bethel they sat before God until evening, lifting up voices and weeping: "O Lord God of Israel, why is this come to pass in Israel, that there should be to day one tribe lacking in Israel?" They rose early, built an altar, and offered burnt offerings and peace offerings.

They inquired who from the tribes had not come to the assembly at Mizpeh, for they had made a great oath that any who did not come up to the Lord at Mizpeh would surely be put to death. Israel grieved for Benjamin, a tribe cut off from Israel. They asked how to provide wives for the survivors, since they had sworn not to give their daughters.

Discovering that none from Jabesh-gilead had come to the assembly, they sent 12,000 valiant men with orders to smite Jabesh-gilead with the sword—every man and every woman who had lain with a man. They found 400 young virgins who had not known a man and brought them to the camp at Shiloh in Canaan. They sent to the 600 Benjamites at the rock of Rimmon, proclaiming peace. Benjamin returned, and they gave them the women saved from Jabesh-gilead. But there were not enough—200 remained without wives.

The elders asked how to provide wives for the rest, since Benjamin's women were destroyed. The inheritance must remain; yet they could not give their own daughters. They devised a plan: the annual feast of the Lord at Shiloh was coming. They told the Benjamites: "Go and lie in wait in the vineyards; and see, and, behold, if the daughters of Shiloh come out to dance in dances, then come ye out of the vineyards, and catch you every man his wife of the daughters of Shiloh, and go to the land of Benjamin." If the fathers or brothers complained, they would say: "Be favourable unto them for our sakes: because we reserved not to each man his wife in the war: for ye did not give unto them at this time, that ye should be guilty."

The Benjamites did so, taking wives according to their number from the dancers, carrying them off to their inheritance. They rebuilt the cities and dwelt there. The children of Israel departed each to his tribe and family, to his own inheritance.

"In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes."
