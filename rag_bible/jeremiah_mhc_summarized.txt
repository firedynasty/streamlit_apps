I notice that you've provided Matthew Henry's introduction to the Book of Jeremiah, but this appears to be Chapter 0 (the general introduction) rather than a specific chapter commentary. Let me summarize Henry's introductory observations about Jeremiah:

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry establishes Jeremiah as fundamentally a **prophet of judgment and warning**. He characterizes Jeremiah as "a reproving prophet, was sent in God's name to tell Jacob of their sins and to warn them of the judgments of God that were coming upon them." Henry emphasizes that Jeremiah ministered during a time of spiritual decline, continuing "through all the wicked reigns that followed" Josiah's good reign, highlighting the progressive deterioration of Israel's spiritual condition.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons from Jeremiah's character and ministry:

**Early Dedication**: Henry notes that Jeremiah "began young" and draws the application that "it is good for a man to bear the yoke in his youth, the yoke both of service and of affliction." This emphasizes the value of early commitment to God's service.

**Perseverance Through Difficulty**: Henry observes that "when we set out for the service of God, though the wind may then be fair and favourable, we know not how soon it may turn and be tempestuous," teaching that faithful servants must prepare for changing circumstances.

**Plain Speaking**: Henry notes that Jeremiah's "style or manner of speaking is more plain and rough" because "those that are sent to discover sin ought to lay aside the enticing words of man's wisdom. Plain-dealing is best when we are dealing with sinners to bring them to repentance."

## Notable Characteristics of Jeremiah

Henry identifies five key aspects of Jeremiah's ministry:

1. **Early calling** - began young
2. **Long service** - continued 40-50 years  
3. **Reproving ministry** - confronted sin directly
4. **Mourning disposition** - "the weeping prophet"
5. **Suffering experience** - "persecuted by his own people more than any of them"

## Cross-References and Connections

Henry makes several scriptural connections:
- Links Jeremiah's youth to **Lamentations 3:27**
- Compares him to **Isaiah's** calling and purification
- Connects him to **Jesus as "man of sorrows"** (referencing Matthew 16:14)
- Parallels the Jews' treatment of Jeremiah with their later treatment of Christ and the apostles (**1 Thessalonians 2:15-16**)

Henry concludes with pastoral application: though Jeremiah's prophecies "were so little regarded by the men of that generation," this should motivate modern readers to "regard them the more; for they are written for our learning too, and for warning to us and to our land."

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah 1: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Predestination and Calling**: Henry emphasizes God's sovereign election of Jeremiah "before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee." He draws parallels to Paul's calling (Galatians 1:15) and notes that "what God has designed men for he will call them to; for his purposes cannot be frustrated." The phrase "Propheta nascitur, non fit--Original endowment, not education, makes a prophet" captures Henry's view of divine appointment.

**God's Faithfulness Despite Human Failure**: A central theme is God's persistence with an impenitent people. Henry observes that despite good King Josiah and prophet Jeremiah working together, "there is no symptom more threatening to any people than fruitless attempts of reformation. Josiah and Jeremiah would have healed them, but they would not be healed."

**Divine Authority Over Nations**: Jeremiah's commission extends beyond Israel to surrounding nations, showing God's sovereignty over all kingdoms through His prophetic word, not political power.

## Key Practical Applications

**Overcoming Youth and Inadequacy**: Henry addresses Jeremiah's protest "I am a child" by noting that "though a sense of our own weakness and insufficiency should make us go humbly about our work, yet it should not make us draw back from it when God calls us to it."

**Courage in Ministry**: Ministers must "not be afraid of the face of man" when delivering God's messages, even unpleasant ones. Henry encourages boldness through God's presence: "if God be for thee, who can be against thee?"

**The Value of Prophetic Voices**: Henry applies this to contemporary situations, noting Jeremiah "is still in his writings a prophet to the nations (to our nation among the rest)" warning about "national judgments" for "national sins."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3 (The Inscription)**: Henry notes the 40-year parallel between Moses in the wilderness and Jeremiah in the land, marking God's patience before judgment. He identifies this as "a special mark" on Judah's final 40 years of impenitency.

**Verse 5 (Predestination)**: Henry emphasizes three principles: "(1.) The great Creator knows what use to make of every man before he makes him... (2.) What God has designed men for he will call them to... (3.) There is a particular purpose and providence of God conversant about his prophets and ministers."

**Verses 11-16 (The Visions)**: Though truncated in the provided text, Henry introduces these as "signifying the approaching ruin of Judah and Jerusalem by the Chaldeans."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry provides extensive biblical connections:
- **Deuteronomy 18:15, 18**: Christ as the ultimate prophet God raises up
- **2 Chronicles 34:3**: Josiah's reforms beginning in his 12th year
- **Galatians 1:15**: Paul's similar calling from the womb
- **Romans 9:21**: God's sovereign right over His creation
- **Exodus 4:14**: Moses' reluctant calling
- **Matthew 10:19**: God providing words in the hour of need
- **Acts 18:10**: Divine presence with ministers

Henry's commentary emphasizes that God's calling transcends human limitations and that faithful ministry requires dependence on divine enablement rather than human qualification. The historical context serves as both encouragement for ministers and warning for nations that resist God's word.

# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 2

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents this chapter as Jeremiah's first sermon after ordination, focusing on the devastating charge of **spiritual adultery and ingratitude against Israel**. The central theological theme is **covenant unfaithfulness** - Israel's abandonment of their exclusive relationship with God for idolatry. Henry frames this as both a breach of marriage covenant (using espousals language) and a violation of God's paternal care.

The commentary emphasizes **God's unchanging character versus human fickleness**. Henry notes this represents something "without precedent, that a nation should change their god," highlighting the theological absurdity of exchanging the true God for false deities. The violation is presented as both unprecedented in history and irrational in logic.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws powerful parallels to **contemporary spiritual idolatry**: "O that by meditating on this chapter we might be brought to repent of our spiritual idolatries, giving that place in our souls to the world and the flesh which should have been reserved for God only!"

He emphasizes the importance of **remembering God's past faithfulness** as a means of conviction and restoration. Ministers are encouraged to "take out a fresh commission" through faith and prayer, ensuring their message aligns with God's Word.

The commentary warns against **promising beginnings without perseverance**: "Those that begin well and promise fair, but do not perform and persevere, will justly be upbraided with their hopeful and promising beginnings."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 2**: Henry interprets "the kindness of thy youth, the love of thine espousals" as both Israel's early devotion to God and God's initial love for Israel. He notes how God remembers even imperfect early faithfulness: "though it was but little love that they showed him, he took it kindly."

**Verse 3**: "Israel was holiness unto the LORD, and the first-fruits of his increase" - Henry explains this as God's special appropriation of Israel, making them the first constituted church, with God defending them as one would protect dedicated first-fruits.

**Verse 5**: The rhetorical question "What iniquity have your fathers found in me?" Henry uses to demonstrate God's perfect justice: "Those that forsake God cannot say that he has ever given them any provocation to do so."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to **Revelation 2:4-5** regarding returning to "first love," and **Galatians 4:15** about early spiritual enthusiasm. He references **Hosea 11:1** ("When Israel was a child, then I loved him") and **Isaiah 62:5** about God's espousal love.

The wilderness journey connects to **Psalm 23:4-5** (walking through the valley, God preparing a table), **Exodus 23:22** (God as enemy to their enemies), and **Luke 22:28** (Christ commending disciples for continuing with him).

Henry concludes that "the ways of the Lord are undoubtedly equal; all the iniquity is in our ways," demonstrating God's perfect righteousness in contrast to human unfaithfulness.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 3: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Jeremiah 3 as a powerful contrast between God's justice and mercy. The central theological theme revolves around **God's covenant faithfulness despite Israel's spiritual adultery**. Henry emphasizes that "as sin abounded grace did much more abound," showcasing God's extraordinary mercy that transcends human limitations.

The **marriage metaphor** dominates Henry's interpretation, where Israel's idolatry represents marital unfaithfulness. He notes that while human law (Deuteronomy 24:4) forbids a divorced husband from remarrying an adulterous wife, God transcends this standard: "God has not tied himself by the laws which he made for us... he will be more kind to Israel, for the sake of his covenant with them, than ever any injured husband was to an adulterous wife; for in receiving penitents, as much as in any thing, **he is God and not man**."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**On Genuine Repentance**: He emphasizes the importance of **specific confession**: "In repentance it is good to make sorrowful reflections upon the particular acts of sin we have been guilty of, and the several places and companies where it has been committed, that we may give glory to God and take shame to ourselves."

**On the Progression of Sin**: Henry warns about spiritual desensitization, noting that "those that have an adulterer's heart, if they indulge that, will come at length to have a whore's forehead, void of all shame and modesty." He identifies that **"those are past shame (we say) are past hope."**

**On Divine Discipline**: He observes God's gentle corrections as opportunities for repentance, contrasting God's withholding of rain with the destruction of Sodom, showing divine patience in judgment.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-5**: Henry highlights the shocking nature of God's invitation to return despite Israel's "many lovers." He emphasizes Israel's shameless pursuit of idolatry, noting they "waited either to court new gods to come among them (the newer the better)."

**Verses 6-11**: The comparison between backsliding Israel and treacherous Judah receives significant attention. Henry notes this sermon was delivered during Josiah's reformation, when "he was hearty, but the people were not sincere in their compliance with it." The hypocrisy of Judah made them worse than Israel, who at least didn't pretend righteousness while sinning.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry makes extensive scriptural connections throughout his commentary:

- **Genesis 38:14** - Comparing Israel's seeking idols to Tamar's harlot disguise
- **Proverbs 9:14-15** - Illustrating Israel's active solicitation of false worship
- **Deuteronomy 24:4** - The divorce law that magnifies God's mercy in receiving Israel back
- **Hosea 2:7** - The return to the "first husband" theme
- **Hosea 14:3** - Finding mercy with God as Father
- **Malachi 2:14** - The husband as "guide of youth"
- **John 8:44** - Warning against calling God "Father" while doing Satan's works

Henry's commentary masterfully weaves these references together to demonstrate both the gravity of Israel's sin and the magnificence of divine grace, showing how God's mercy exceeds all human understanding and legal precedent.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 4: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents several key theological themes in Jeremiah 4. **Divine judgment and mercy** stand at the center, as God offers Israel a path to restoration while warning Judah of impending destruction. Henry emphasizes that **true repentance must be thorough and heartfelt**, not merely external. He interprets the coming Babylonian invasion as God's righteous judgment against persistent idolatry, yet maintains that even imminent judgment can be averted through genuine repentance.

The commentary also explores the **universality of God's plan**, noting that Israel's restoration would influence other nations to "bless themselves in him." Henry sees God's wrath as both terrible and just, kindled specifically by "the evil of their doings."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical applications for believers. He emphasizes that **repentance requires concrete action**: "Do not talk of it, but do it." Using the agricultural metaphor, he instructs that hearts must be cultivated like fallow ground—"We must search into our own hearts, let the word of God divide (as the plough does) between the joints and the marrow."

Regarding oaths and truth-telling, Henry provides practical guidance: swear only by the true God, speak only truth "in righteousness," and do so "solemnly" when judicially required, not in casual conversation. He warns against the dangers of spiritual complacency and emphasizes that external religious observances mean nothing without heart transformation.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry offers rich interpretations throughout the chapter. On verses 1-2, he explains that Israel's return must be complete: "return unto me; make a thorough work of it." For verse 3's "break up your fallow ground," he describes the unconverted heart as "ground untilled, unoccupied...overgrown with thorns and weeds, which are the natural product of the corrupt heart."

Regarding verse 4's call to "circumcise your hearts," Henry emphasizes spiritual over physical circumcision: "Boast not of, and rest not in, the circumcision of the body, for that is but a sign." He interprets the lion imagery of verse 7 as representing Nebuchadnezzar, "this roaring tearing lion, the destroyer of the nations."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects Jeremiah 4 to other biblical passages. He references **Deuteronomy 4:29 and 30:2** regarding restoration after repentance, **Isaiah 65:16** on nations blessing themselves in God, and **Hosea 10:12** on breaking up fallow ground. For his discussion of oath-taking, he cites **Deuteronomy 10:20** and **Matthew 5:34, 37**.

He also draws from **Hebrews 6:7-8** regarding unfruitful ground, **Hebrews 4:12** on God's word dividing hearts, **Joel 2:13** on rending hearts, and **James 1:21** on receiving God's word with meekness. These cross-references demonstrate Henry's understanding of scripture's unified message about repentance, judgment, and restoration.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah 5: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry structures Jeremiah 5 around three core theological concepts: **universal corruption, divine justice, and tempered mercy**. The central theme is Jerusalem's complete moral decay, which Henry describes as resembling "the old world, in which all flesh had corrupted their way." He emphasizes that both poor and rich are culpable—the poor through willful ignorance, the rich through rebellious knowledge.

The commentary highlights **God's holiness and justice** as requiring judgment: "when God punishes sin, he is said to visit for it, or enquire into it; for he weighs the cause before he passes sentence." Henry notes that punishment comes from both God's justice and His holiness, stating "this is intimated in that, 'Shall not my soul be avenged on such a nation as this?'"

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for his readers:

**On the danger of prosperity:** He warns that spiritual adultery followed material blessing—"When they were fed to the full, then their lusts grew thus furious." He notes "Fulness of bread was fuel to the fire of Sodom's lusts," advocating that "fasting would help to tame the unruly evil."

**On the universality of corruption:** Henry observes that social class provides no moral immunity—"The poor are weak, the rich are wilful, and so neither do their duty." This challenges assumptions about virtue correlating with education or status.

**On religious hypocrisy:** He condemns those who maintain religious appearances while living wickedly: "they honour him with their lips, but their hearts are far from him."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-2:** Henry interprets the search for one righteous person as showing God's readiness to forgive: "So pleasing would it be to God to find any such that for their sake he would pardon the city." However, even religious profession proves hollow—they "say, The Lord liveth" but "swear falsely."

**Verses 4-5:** Henry provides nuanced analysis of class-based corruption. The poor "are foolish...they know not the way of the LORD," while the educated rich "know the way of the Lord...yet they are too stiff to stoop to his government."

**Verses 7-8:** The commentary vividly describes moral degradation: "they became perfect beasts...like high-fed horses, they neighed every one after his neighbour's wife." Henry emphasizes how spiritual unfaithfulness leads to physical immorality.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry skillfully weaves biblical cross-references throughout:

- **Micah 7:1-2** and **Psalm 12:1** regarding the scarcity of faithful people
- **Isaiah 59:14** on truth falling in the streets
- **Psalm 2:3** for the rebellion of the great against divine authority
- **Romans 1:32** concerning God's judgment on sin
- The **Sodom narrative** as a parallel for seeking righteous remnants

## Conclusion

Henry presents Jeremiah 5 as a comprehensive indictment of society's moral collapse, while maintaining hope in God's mercy. He concludes with the note that "God would in the midst of wrath remember mercy, and not utterly destroy them," showing divine grace even amid deserved judgment. The commentary serves both as historical analysis and contemporary warning about the consequences of abandoning moral and spiritual foundations.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 6: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Jeremiah 6 as a divine judgment narrative with three central theological themes. First, **God's sovereign use of foreign powers** as instruments of judgment—the Chaldean army serves as God's commissioned agent, with Henry noting that "they have no power but what is given them from above." Second, **the inevitability of divine justice** when sin reaches its fullness, particularly emphasizing that "it is in vain to contend with God's judgments." Third, **God's reluctant departure from His people**, beautifully expressed in verse 8 where Henry observes God's "tender affection," noting that "his very soul had been joined to them, and nothing but sin could disjoin it."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for Christian living. He warns that "the more we indulge ourselves in the pleasures of this life the more we disfit ourselves for the troubles of this life," comparing Jerusalem to a "comely and delicate woman" unprepared for hardship. He challenges believers' spiritual urgency, asking: "O that we were thus eager in our spiritual work and warfare, thus afraid of losing time...in taking the kingdom of heaven by violence!" 

Most memorably, Henry applies verse 7's description of sin flowing "as a fountain casts out her waters" to the human heart, explaining it represents how naturally and constantly wickedness flows from our corrupt nature—"always flowing, and yet always full."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides rich exposition throughout. On verses 1-6, he notes the irony of people fleeing from city to country and back, observing that such panic is "all in vain when evil pursues sinners with commission." 

Regarding verse 14's "Peace, peace; when there is no peace," he exposes the false prophets who "healed...the hurt of the daughter of my people slightly." 

His treatment of verse 16's call to "ask for the old paths" emphasizes returning to proven spiritual foundations, while verse 30's "reprobate silver" becomes a metaphor for those who have become spiritually worthless despite God's refining attempts.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry weaves numerous scriptural connections throughout his commentary. He links the description of Jerusalem's delicacy to Deuteronomy 28:56, connects God's use of foreign powers to Isaiah 10:6-7, and references Matthew 23:38 regarding God's departure leading to desolation. The warning about God's soul having "no pleasure" in those who draw back connects to Hebrews 10:38, while the fountain imagery echoes broader biblical themes about the heart's corruption found throughout Scripture.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 7: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes the **invalidity of external religious privileges without internal righteousness**. The central theme is that possessing the temple and attending its services cannot substitute for genuine obedience to God. He stresses that "to obey is better than sacrifice" (referencing 1 Samuel 15:22), arguing that God rejects the sacrifices of those who persist in disobedience.

The commentary warns against **false security in religious externals**. Henry notes how the people repeatedly chanted "The temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord" as a protective mantra, believing their proximity to the temple guaranteed God's favor despite their sins. He observes: "The privileges of a form of godliness are often the pride and confidence of those that are strangers and enemies to the power of it."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications:

1. **True reformation must be comprehensive**: "They must thoroughly amend; in making good, they must make good their ways and doings; they must reform with resolution, and it must be a universal, constant, preserving reformation—not partial, but entire—not hypocritical, but sincere."

2. **Justice and mercy in daily conduct**: Practical righteousness includes executing fair judgment, not oppressing "the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow," and avoiding the shedding of innocent blood.

3. **Warning against self-deception**: Those who "slight the words of truth, which would profit them, take shelter in words of falsehood, which cannot profit them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On **verses 1-2**: Henry notes the strategic placement of this sermon "in the gate of the Lord's house" during a feast when many gathered, emphasizing that "even those that profess religion have need to be preached to."

On **verse 4**: The threefold repetition of "temple of the Lord" represents the people's desperate clinging to external religious symbols as security.

On **verses 9-10**: Henry highlights the absurdity of combining gross immorality with temple worship, asking: "Will you steal, murder, and commit adultery...and then come and stand before me in this house?"

On **verse 11**: Jesus later quotes this "den of robbers" passage, which Henry connects to the principle that religious buildings become corrupted when used by corrupt people.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry makes several biblical connections:
- **Matthew 3:8-9**: Comparing this message to John the Baptist's call to "bring forth fruits meet for repentance" rather than trusting in Abrahamic lineage
- **Philippians 3:3**: The need to "worship him in the spirit, and to have no confidence in the flesh"
- **1 Samuel 15:22**: The fundamental principle that obedience supersedes sacrifice
- **Zephaniah 3:11**: Reference to those who "are haughty because of the holy mountain"

Henry emphasizes that this chapter's message parallels Christ's later cleansing of the temple, showing the consistency of God's demand for authentic worship across both testaments. The commentary serves as a timeless warning against substituting religious formalism for genuine spiritual transformation.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 8: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Jeremiah 8 as a profound demonstration of God's righteous judgment against persistent rebellion. The chapter explores **divine justice versus human stubbornness**, showing how God's wrath, though severe, is perfectly justified by Judah's willful impenitence. 

Henry emphasizes that this destruction reveals **the futility of idolatry**—the very celestial bodies Judah worshipped will witness their humiliation as "their dead bodies be cast, and left to putrefy...and the sun's shining upon them will but make them the more noisome and offensive." The commentary highlights how **sin is fundamentally irrational**, contrasting human behavior with both natural instincts and divine expectations.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**On Spiritual Priorities**: "Let this cure us of the inordinate love of life, that the case may be such that it may become a burden and terror." Earthly attachments can become sources of misery when not properly ordered.

**On Repentance**: True repentance requires honest self-examination, beginning with the question "What have I done?" Henry notes that many avoid even this first step toward spiritual restoration.

**On False Security**: He warns against trusting in religious knowledge without corresponding righteousness, noting how the scribes' wisdom became "vain" when divorced from obedience.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: Henry describes how death itself becomes perverted—no longer offering rest but bringing additional shame through desecrated graves. The irony is striking: Judah's idols become unwitting spectators to their devotees' degradation.

**Verses 4-6**: The commentary powerfully contrasts natural human behavior with spiritual obstinacy: "Shall men fall and not arise?...Why then has this people of Jerusalem slidden back by a perpetual backsliding?" Henry notes God "hearkened and heard" expectantly, like one hoping for repentance.

**Verse 7**: The famous comparison with migrating birds emphasizes that "my people know not the judgment of the LORD"—even animals show more wisdom in following their Creator's design.

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry enriches his commentary with extensive scriptural connections: **Psalm 141:7** regarding scattered bones, **Job 33:27** about God watching human responses to conviction, and **Psalm 32:5** concerning David's confession. He references **2 Kings 23:18** regarding Josiah's protection of godly remains and connects the text to **Job 39:21** in describing the war horse's fearless charge into battle—illustrating sinners' reckless pursuit of destruction.

The commentary demonstrates how Jeremiah 8 fits within the broader biblical narrative of covenant unfaithfulness and divine justice while pointing toward the necessity of genuine repentance.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 9: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Jeremiah as a model prophet who "neither rejoiced at iniquity nor was glad at calamities," demonstrating the proper balance between faithfully reproving sin and genuinely lamenting its consequences. The chapter reveals God's justice in judgment while emphasizing that such judgment brings genuine grief to both the prophet and God himself.

The commentary emphasizes the progressive nature of sin, showing how Judah proceeded "from evil to evil" until deception became their second nature. Henry notes that "through deceit they refuse to know me, saith the LORD," illustrating how sin creates a barrier to divine relationship. The theological heart lies in verse 7's divine lament: "how shall I do for the daughter of my people?"—portraying God's reluctant but necessary judgment.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for believers. Regarding weeping for sin, he observes: "While we find our hearts such fountains of sin, it is fit that our eyes should be fountains of tears." He calls Christians to conform to "the temper of the climate" in this "vale of tears," emphasizing that "Blessed are those that mourn, for they shall be comforted hereafter."

On social relationships, Henry warns against the corruption that makes even family bonds untrustworthy, noting how "things have come to an ill pass indeed when a man cannot put confidence in his own brother." He emphasizes courage in truth-telling, stating we must be "valiant for the truth" and "not be daunted by the opposition given to it."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 1**: Henry explains that "the same word in Hebrew signifies both the eye and a fountain, as if in this land of sorrows our eyes were designed rather for weeping than seeing." Jeremiah's grief was primarily public, not personal—weeping "for the slain of the daughter of his people."

**Verses 2-3**: The prophet's desire for wilderness solitude reflects not cowardice but righteous revulsion at sin. Henry notes this "expresses the holy indignation he had against their abominable wickedness," comparing Jeremiah's situation to Lot's vexation in Sodom.

**Verses 4-6**: Henry observes the systematic breakdown of social trust, where tongues are "bent like their bow for lies" with deliberate craft, while people are "not valiant for the truth upon the earth."

## Cross-References to Scripture

Henry connects the passage to several biblical texts. He references Psalm 120:5 regarding Jeremiah's feeling like one who "sojourns in Mesech," and 2 Peter 2:7-8 concerning righteous souls being "vexed from day to day" by surrounding wickedness.

The commentary links to Psalm 58:3 about the wicked being "estranged from the womb, speaking lies," and Isaiah 59:14-15 where "truth has fallen in the land." Henry also references Jesus' teaching about mourning (Matthew 5:4) and connects the social deception to Jacob's name meaning "supplanter."

The chapter concludes with Henry's exposition of verses 23-26, emphasizing that true security comes only from trusting "God only" rather than human wisdom, strength, or religious privileges like circumcision—a theme that resonates throughout Scripture's teaching on false confidence versus genuine faith.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 10: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies this prophecy as delivered after the first Babylonian captivity, addressing both the exiled and those remaining in Judah. The central theological theme contrasts the **living God of Israel with dead idols**, emphasizing God's absolute sovereignty and the folly of idolatry.

The commentary presents God as "the one only living and true God" who is incomparable to heathen deities. Henry emphasizes God as Creator who "made the earth by his power" and "established the world by his wisdom," contrasting divine omnipotence with idols that "cannot speak" and "must be carried."

A key theological insight is that idolatry represents not merely wrong worship, but fundamental irrationality - those who worship idols become "brutish in his knowledge" and "altogether brutish and foolish."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Separation from worldly customs**: "Learn not the way of the heathen" - Christians must not conform to pagan practices or "let any of their customs steal in among you." This warns against syncretism in worship.

**Freedom from superstitious fear**: Believers should not "be dismayed at the signs of heaven" like astrology or omens, since "the stars in their courses fight not against any that are at peace with God."

**Confident trust in God's providence**: Since God controls all things, His people need not fear created things or superstitious practices that terrify unbelievers.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 3-5**: Henry emphasizes the absurdity of idol-making - trees cut from forests, decorated with gold and silver, yet requiring nails to prevent falling. They are "upright as the palm-tree, but speak not" and "cannot go."

**Verses 6-7**: God's incomparability is stressed - "among all the wise men of the nations...there is none like unto thee, O King of nations."

**Verses 11-13**: Henry highlights God's creative power versus idols' impotence: "The gods that have not made the heavens and earth...shall perish," while God "made the earth by his power."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to other biblical texts:
- **Deuteronomy 12:29-31** on avoiding heathen worship practices
- **Isaiah 44:12** on the process of idol-making  
- **Psalm 21:3** regarding "fine gold" from Uphaz
- **Romans 1:21-28** on humanity's descent into idolatry despite knowing God
- **1 Corinthians 1:21** - "The world by wisdom knew not God"
- **Jude 10** on corrupting natural knowledge

Henry notes that Isaiah similarly "exposed the sottishness of idolaters" when prophesying about Babylonian captivity, showing consistent biblical opposition to idolatry. The commentary demonstrates how both affliction and God's word work together to cure people of idolatrous tendencies.

This passage ultimately calls believers to exclusive devotion to the living God while exposing the fundamental irrationality of trusting in anything created rather than the Creator.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah 11 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Covenant Theology**: Henry emphasizes that God's relationship with Israel was fundamentally covenantal, based on conditional obedience. The commentary stresses that "obedience was the rent reserved by the lease, with a power to re-enter for non-payment." This covenant included both blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience, making it clear that God's promises were not unconditional.

**Divine Judgment and Justice**: Henry presents God's judgment as perfectly just, given Israel's persistent rebellion despite continual warnings. He notes that "God had been, in one way or other, at sundry times and in divers manners, protesting to them the necessity of obedience" for nearly 1000 years.

**Conspiracy Against God**: The chapter reveals a systematic rebellion - "a conspiracy is found among them" - showing that Israel's disobedience was not accidental but deliberate and organized across generations and regions.

## Key Practical Applications

**Moral Over Ceremonial**: Henry emphasizes that "the thing God insisted upon was obedience, which was better than sacrifice." True religion requires moral obedience, not mere ritual observance.

**Individual Responsibility**: The warning that "cursed be the man that obeyeth not the words of this covenant" applies to individuals, not just the nation collectively. Each person must personally respond to God's covenant.

**Consequences of Rebellion**: Henry warns that persistent disobedience leads to divine abandonment: "I will not hearken unto them" when they cry for help. False gods "shall not save them at all in the time of their trouble."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-7**: Henry notes the progression from God's original covenant terms through persistent warnings. Egypt is called an "iron furnace" - God redeemed them from "perfect slavery" to bring them into His service, which is "perfect freedom."

**Verses 8-10**: The phrase "walked every one in the imagination of their evil heart" shows how rebellion becomes systematic when people reject God's authority and "every man did as his fancy and humour led him."

**Verses 11-14**: Henry observes the irony that those who served multiple gods would find none able to help: "according to the number of thy cities were thy gods, O Judah" - yet abundance of false gods provides no security.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to **Luke 1:74-75** regarding God's redemption purpose, **Deuteronomy 30:15** on the choice between life and death, and **Numbers 32:14** describing successive generations as "a seed of evil-doers." He also references the covenant ceremonies at Mount Ebal and the people's initial consent to God's terms.

The commentary emphasizes that Jeremiah's message aligned perfectly with Moses' law, showing the consistency of divine revelation across centuries. Henry concludes that "the words of the covenant shall not fall to the ground" - God will fulfill both His promises and His warnings based on human response to His covenant terms.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah 12: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry identifies the central theological tension in this chapter as the **problem of theodicy** - why the wicked prosper while the righteous suffer. The prophet's struggle represents a universal human temptation that "has been a temptation to many wise and good men." Henry emphasizes that even in doubt, believers must maintain foundational truth: **"Righteous art thou, O Lord!"** 

The commentary distinguishes between legitimate spiritual inquiry and sinful quarreling with God. Henry notes: "We may not strive with our Maker, but we may reason with him." This establishes proper boundaries for questioning divine providence.

A key theological insight concerns **hypocrisy versus true faith**. The wicked are characterized as those for whom God is "near in their mouth, and far from their reins" - displaying outward piety while lacking genuine heart devotion.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

**Preparation for Greater Trials**: God's response to Jeremiah - "If thou hast run with the footmen, and they have wearied thee, then how canst thou contend with horses?" - teaches that present sufferings prepare us for greater challenges ahead. **Dealing with Family Opposition**: Henry notes that "God's faithful servants must not think it at all strange if their foes be those of their own house," applying this to ministers and believers facing opposition from unexpected quarters.

**Heart Examination**: The commentary emphasizes that "we are as our hearts are, and our hearts are good or bad according as they are, or are not, towards God."

**Verse 1**: Henry highlights that Jeremiah begins his complaint by affirming God's righteousness, demonstrating proper approach to difficult questions about divine justice.

**Verse 2**: The prosperity of the wicked is described using agricultural metaphors - they are "planted," "take root," "grow," and "bring forth fruit" - yet remain spiritually barren.

**Verses 5-6**: God's answer serves as both validation and correction - acknowledging Jeremiah's legitimate grievances while challenging his perspective and preparing him for greater opposition.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 13: Summary

Matthew Henry presents three primary theological themes through symbolic acts and prophetic warnings. ## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry addresses whether Jeremiah's journey to Euphrates was literal or visionary, concluding it was likely literal, demonstrating the prophet's "signal proof of his obsequiousness to his God, to shame the stubbornness of a disobedient people." 

On verse 11, he notes the intimate covenant relationship: God caused Israel to "cleave to him by the law he gave them, the prophets he sent among them, and the favours which in his providence he showed them."

Regarding the wine bottles (v. 12-14), Henry explains this was likely a common proverb that the people mocked, not understanding its prophetic significance about coming judgment.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 18 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents two central theological truths through the potter's house metaphor. **First**, God possesses "incontestable authority and irresistible ability to form and fashion kingdoms and nations as he pleases." **Second**, divine justice operates conditionally based on human response. Henry explains that "national repentance will stop the progress of judgments," while conversely, "sin is the great mischief-maker between God and a people" that can halt God's blessings. This establishes a clear principle: God's dealings with nations depend on their moral and spiritual condition.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers and nations. He emphasizes that "sincere conversion from the evil of sin will be an effectual prevention of the evil of punishment." When God threatens judgment, repentance can redirect His purposes toward mercy, as demonstrated historically with Nineveh.

For ministers, Henry notes they should "learn thereby to speak more plainly and familiarly to people about the things of God" by observing everyday affairs like pottery-making. He warns that obstinate refusal to reform leads to spiritual hardening, where people declare "we will walk after our own devices" - representing "the worst of slaveries" to one's lusts.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-6**: The potter's house visit wasn't merely observational but revelatory - "there I will cause thee to hear my words." Henry notes that when clay proved unsuitable for one vessel, the potter "made it again another vessel," illustrating how "if he be not glorified by them, he will be glorified upon them."

**Verses 7-10**: Henry presents the conditional nature of divine judgment and blessing. God's "repenting" represents changing His course based on human response, not changing His character.

**Verses 11-17**: Applied to Judah's situation, Henry shows God "frames evil against you" like a potter shapes clay, yet offers opportunity for repentance before proceeding with judgment.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes. He references **Romans 9:21** on God's potter-like authority, **Isaiah 64:8** on humanity as clay, and **Job 12:23** on God's sovereignty over nations. He cites **Proverbs 16:4** - "The Lord has made all things for himself, yea, even the wicked for the day of evil" - to support divine sovereignty.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah 22: Summary

Henry shows how **God's judgment operates through natural consequences**—when rulers abandon justice, their kingdoms inevitably collapse. The phrase "God never casts any off until they first cast him off" encapsulates this principle.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for leadership and governance:

**For Rulers**: "The most effectual way to preserve the dignity of the government is to do the duty of it." Kings must actively defend the oppressed and "deliver the spoiled out of the hand of their oppressors" while avoiding violence against "the stranger, fatherless, and widow."

**For All Authority**: Henry warns against corruption under "colour of law and justice"—the greatest injustice occurs when those meant to protect become oppressors. He contrasts Josiah's simple lifestyle with Jehoiakim's extravagance, arguing that true kingship means serving justice, not pursuing luxury.

**General Principle**: "Sin will be the ruin of the houses of princes as well as of mean men." No earthly status exempts anyone from divine judgment.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides memorable expositions throughout:

**Verses 1-9**: He notes how God's judgment comes through prepared "destroyers," observing that "when destruction is designed destroyers are prepared...long before." The image of choice cedars being cut down and burned illustrates how even the strongest institutions fall when corrupted.

**Verses 10-12**: Henry offers striking counsel about grief: "Weep not for the dead...but weep sore for him that goeth away." He explains that "dying saints may be justly envied, while living sinners are justly pitied"—Josiah died in honor while Jehoahaz would "live and die in disgrace and misery."

**Verses 15-16**: The contrast between father and son produces Henry's famous question: "was not this to know me? saith the LORD"—referring to Josiah's care for the poor and needy as the essence of knowing God.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry enriches his commentary with extensive biblical connections:

- **Psalm 82:3** for the duty to defend the injured
- **Exodus 22:21-22** regarding protection of widows and orphans  
- **Hebrews 6:13** explaining God's oath-making
- **Job 21:29** on learning from travelers who witness God's judgments
- **2 Kings 23:34 and 2 Chronicles 36:4** for historical context on the kings' fates
- **Luke 23:28** drawing parallels between different occasions for weeping

The commentary demonstrates Henry's characteristic integration of theological principle with practical wisdom, showing how divine justice operates through historical circumstances while maintaining hope in God's ultimate righteousness.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah 24: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Jeremiah 24 as a profound illustration of **divine discrimination in judgment**. The central theme is that "God knew how to distinguish, how to separate, between the precious and the vile." Henry emphasizes that **the same providence affects people differently**—what appears as universal calamity actually serves distinct purposes for different groups.

The vision of good and bad figs represents how **early suffering can be redemptive**. Henry notes that "we cannot determine of God's love or hatred by all that is before us" and that "early suffering sometimes proves for the best." This challenges assumptions about who suffers first being the worst sinners.

A crucial theological insight is that **affliction serves different purposes**: for the godly, it's "a correcting rod in the hand of a tender Father," while for the wicked, it's "an avenging sword in the hand of a righteous Judge."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons:

**Don't judge by appearances**: "We must not conclude concerning the first and greatest sufferers that they were the worst and greatest sinners." The timing of suffering doesn't indicate God's favor or disfavor.

**Trust God's purposes in affliction**: Even disgraceful captivity was intended "for their good." Henry explains that through affliction, people "were convinced of sin, humbled under the hand of God, weaned from the world, made serious, taught to pray, and turned from their iniquity."

**Recognize God's active involvement**: "Whoever were the instruments of it, he ordered and directed it." God personally oversees the afflictions of his people, like "a father corrects the child with his own hand."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 1-3**: The vision occurs after Jeconiah's captivity, when valuable people like Ezekiel and Daniel were taken. Henry notes the carpenters and smiths were specifically taken either because "the Chaldeans needed ingenious men" or to leave the Jews "unable to fortify their cities."

**Verse 5**: Henry emphasizes that God personally sent the captives "into the land of the Chaldeans for their good," highlighting divine intentionality in suffering.

**Verse 7**: The promise of "a heart to know me" represents not mere intellectual knowledge but "convictions of the practical judgment directing and governing the will and affections." This knowledge leads to returning to God "with their whole heart."

**Verses 8-10**: The bad figs represent those who remained proud and secure, thinking they had "acted prudently" by avoiding captivity or fleeing to Egypt.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to several biblical texts:

- **Judges 9:11** - comparing people to fig trees capable of serving God and man
- **Micah 7:1** - referencing first-ripe figs as most acceptable  
- **Psalm 119:67, 71** - "it was good for them that they were afflicted"
- **Ezekiel 1:12 and 11:3** - referencing Ezekiel among the valuable captives and the pride of those remaining
- **Lamentations 3:27** - early correction being beneficial
- **Psalm 111:10** - true understanding coming through obedience

Henry's commentary transforms this vision into a comprehensive theology of redemptive suffering, showing how God's judgments, while appearing universal, actually serve his particular purposes for his people's ultimate good.

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers. First, he emphasizes that "those that are delivered out of spiritual bondage must make it appear that they are so by giving up themselves to the service of Christ." Deliverance must lead to dedicated service.

Second, he applies the principle that suffering often correlates with sin: "Are our sorrows multiplied at any time and do they increase? We must acknowledge that it is because our sins have been multiplied and they have increased."

On verses 1-3, Henry notes God's command to write the prophecies "in a book, to be carefully preserved in the archives," emphasizing their enduring value for future generations and referencing how "Daniel understood by these books when the captivity was about coming to an end."

For verse 9's promise of serving "David their king," Henry provides rich messianic interpretation: "David was an illustrious type of him both in his humiliation and in his exaltation...God is often in the New Testament said to have raised up Jesus, raised him up as a King."

Henry's exposition masterfully weaves together the historical context of Babylonian exile with the prophetic hope of messianic restoration, providing both comfort for immediate circumstances and eternal hope through Christ's ultimate kingship.

# Summary: Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah 33

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry views Jeremiah 33 as confirmation of God's promises to restore the Jews after their exile, but emphasizes that these promises have "both in type and tendency, a reference as far forward as to the gospel church." The restoration serves as a type pointing toward Christ's kingdom.

The theological progression moves from judgment to healing, exile to restoration, and ultimately from the old covenant to the new. Henry sees the promise of the "Branch of righteousness" (v.15) as directly messianic, leading to Christ's eternal kingdom.

## Key Practical Applications

**Prayer and Divine Revelation**: Henry emphasizes that "promises are given, not to supersede, but to quicken and encourage prayer." The command to "Call unto me, and I will answer thee" (v.3) shows that God's people must actively seek His comfort through prayer, even when promises exist.

**Comfort in Affliction**: Henry notes that Jeremiah received these encouraging words while imprisoned, teaching that "no confinement can deprive God's people of his presence; no locks nor bars can shut out his gracious visits." Often "as their afflictions abound their consolations much more abound."

**Divine Healing**: Henry applies God's promise to bring "health and cure" both literally to Jerusalem and spiritually to souls through the gospel, which "heals the soul of the diseases it has contracted."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: God as "maker and former" has power to rebuild what He originally created. The "great and mighty things" represent both prophectic revelations and their fulfillment.

**Verses 4-5**: Henry describes Jerusalem's devastating condition - houses "thrown down by the mounts" and filled with "dead bodies of men" - as necessary context for understanding the magnitude of God's promised restoration.

**Verse 6**: "Peace and truth" represent the gospel's central message. Henry explains: "Grace and truth, and abundance of both, come by Jesus Christ. Peace and truth are the life of the soul."

**Verses 7-8**: Restoration includes both physical return and spiritual cleansing. Henry notes the comprehensive nature: cleansing from filth and freedom from guilt through pardoning mercy.

**Verse 11**: The return of "the voice of joy and gladness" and worship demonstrates complete transformation from desolation to celebration.

Henry connects extensively with other biblical passages:
- Links to Jeremiah 32:17 regarding God as maker of heaven and earth
- References Exodus 6:3 for God's name "Jehovah" as the promise-keeping God  
- Cites Psalm 2:8 showing even Christ must ask to receive
- Connects to Ezekiel 36:37 on prayer's necessity despite promises
- Points to John 18:37 and Psalm 85:10 regarding Christ's rule through truth and peace

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah 35: Summary

**Verses 8-10**: Henry marvels at their multigenerational faithfulness: "See the force of tradition, and the influence that antiquity, example, and great names, have upon men."

Henry's commentary transforms this historical narrative into a timeless lesson on faithful obedience, practical wisdom, and the power of consistent example across generations.

# Summary of Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 36

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents Chapter 36 as demonstrating God's patient persistence with His rebellious people through multiple means of communication. The central theme is **divine mercy offering repeated opportunities for repentance** even to those who persistently reject God's word. Henry draws several practical lessons for ministers and believers:

Henry connects this passage to **Ezekiel 2:9-10 and 3:1**, contrasting Ezekiel's visionary scroll (for the prophet's own understanding) with Jeremiah's physical scroll (for public proclamation).

He references **Isaiah 48:8** regarding God's certain foreknowledge despite using language of possibility, and **1 Corinthians 12:21** to illustrate the complementary gifts of Jeremiah and Baruch.

The commentary also draws parallels to **Paul's epistle writing** (Galatians 6:11, Romans 16:22) and his principle that "to preach and write the same thing is safe and profitable" (Philippians 3:1).

Henry concludes that this chapter demonstrates both the patient methodology of divine communication and the tragic progression of human resistance to God's word, serving as both encouragement for faithful ministry and warning against hardened hearts.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah 39: Summary

**On self-inflicted spiritual danger**: Henry notes that "when we thrust ourselves without cause or call into places of temptation, it is just with God to leave us to ourselves." Those who went to Egypt against God's will became more enslaved to idolatry than those obediently carried to Babylon.

**On contentment and submission**: He observes that "those that are fretful and discontented will be uneasy and fond of change wherever they are," warning against the restless pursuit of circumstances God has not ordained.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry provides compelling analysis of the people's shocking rebellion in verses 15-19. Their argument that they prospered when worshiping the "queen of heaven" but suffered when they stopped represents, in Henry's view, **the ultimate perversion of spiritual logic**. He shows how they completely misread cause and effect, attributing their judgments to abandoning idols rather than to the idolatry itself.

The phrase "we will certainly do whatsoever thing goeth forth out of our own mouth" (v. 17) Henry sees as the essence of human rebellion—the determination to follow one's own will regardless of divine command.

## Cross-References to Scripture

Henry connects this passage to **Psalm 137:1** regarding the Babylonian captives who remembered Jerusalem, contrasting them with the Egyptian refugees who "seem to have forgotten." He references **Psalm 34:16** ("the face of the Lord is against those that do evil") when explaining God's opposition to the obstinate.

He also draws from **Nehemiah 13:18, 26** regarding Solomon's wives and their role in introducing idolatry, suggesting a historical pattern of Egyptian influence leading to spiritual compromise.

## Conclusion

Henry's commentary presents this chapter as a sobering study in **spiritual blindness and divine justice**. The people's brazen defiance serves as a warning about sin's power to corrupt judgment itself, while God's unwavering response demonstrates that persistent rebellion ultimately leads to complete destruction, with only a tiny remnant escaping through divine mercy.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah 45: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry presents this brief chapter as a divine message specifically for Baruch, Jeremiah's scribe, yet emphasizes its broader application as "intended for the support and encouragement of all the Lord's people that serve him faithfully and keep closely to him in difficult trying times." The central theological theme revolves around God's pastoral care for even the "meanest and weakest" of His servants, showing that "God despises not any of his servants, but graciously concerns himself for the meanest and weakest, for Baruch the scribe as well as for Jeremiah the prophet."

The commentary explores the tension between worldly expectations and divine calling during times of national crisis. Henry interprets God's rebuke of Baruch as addressing misplaced priorities during judgment: "That which I have built, to be a house for myself, I am breaking down...and dost thou now seek great things for thyself?"

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers facing adversity:

**1. Regarding New Believers:** He notes that "Young beginners in religion, like fresh-water soldiers, are apt to be discouraged with the little difficulties which they commonly meet with at first in the service of God." This serves as both warning and encouragement for spiritual mentors.

**2. On Worldly Expectations:** The commentary warns against excessive attachment to temporal success: "The frowns of the world would not disquiet us as they do if we did not foolishly flatter ourselves with the hopes of its smiles and court and covet them too much."

**3. Regarding Suffering:** Henry criticizes Baruch's response, noting he "should have rejoiced that he was counted worthy to suffer in such a good cause and with such good company, but, instead of that, he is vexed at it."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 3:** Henry analyzes Baruch's complaint ("Woe is me now!") as the response of "a young man setting out in the world" who "stood fair for preferment" but faced unexpected persecution instead of recognition.

**Verses 4-5:** The divine response receives extensive treatment. Henry interprets the metaphor of a sinking ship: "It is absurd for thee to be now painting thy own cabin. Canst thou expect to be high when all are brought low, to be full when all about thee are empty?"

The promise that Baruch's life would be given "for a prey" is explained as narrow escape: "thou shalt escape, though often very narrowly, shalt have thy life, but it shall be as a prey, which is got with much difficulty and danger; thou shalt be saved as by fire."

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry references **Proverbs 24:10** regarding weakness in adversity and connects Baruch's situation to Moses' hasty speech, showing how "God keeps account what we say, even when we speak in haste." He also alludes to **1 Corinthians 3:15** ("saved as by fire") and Jesus' teaching that "the life is more than meat."

The commentary draws parallels between Baruch's experience and that of "fresh-water soldiers" and connects this passage to the broader theme of finding security in God rather than worldly success during times when "every thing is uncertain and perishing."

Henry concludes that life preservation during judgment represents "a signal favour" for which believers should be grateful, even when disappointed in worldly ambitions.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah Chapter 46: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Henry emphasizes God's **universal sovereignty over nations**, noting that "God is King and Judge of nations, knows and will call to an account those who know him not." The commentary highlights how divine judgment extends beyond Israel to encompass all nations, demonstrating that God's authority transcends geographical and religious boundaries.

A central theme is **divine justice versus human pride**. Henry observes how Egypt's arrogant boasting - "I will go up; I will cover the earth" - contrasts sharply with God's purposes. He notes: "They meant one thing, but God meant another; they designed it for the advancement of their dignity...but God designed it for the great abasement and weakening of their kingdom."

The commentary also presents God's **covenant faithfulness to Israel**. Despite judgment on surrounding nations, Henry emphasizes the promise to Jacob: "I will make a full end of all the nations whither I have driven thee: but I will not make a full end of thee."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications for believers:

**Military might proves futile without God's blessing**: "The race is not to the swift nor the battle to the strong. Valiant men are not always victorious." He warns against trusting in human strength rather than divine providence.

**True preparation for conflict requires spiritual readiness**: "It concerns those that go forth to war not only to order the buckler, and harness the horses, but to repent of their sins, and pray to God for his presence with them."

**Pride precedes destruction**: Henry emphasizes how Egypt's overconfidence led to catastrophic defeat, warning that "when men think to magnify themselves by pushing on unrighteous enterprises, let them expect that God will glorify himself by blasting them."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry vividly describes Egypt's **military preparations** (verses 3-4) as elaborate but ultimately futile, comparing their army's advance to the flooding Nile (verses 7-8). He notes the irony that Egypt's extensive confederate forces from Ethiopia, Libya, and Lydia cannot withstand divine judgment.

Regarding the **defeat at Carchemish** (verses 5-6), Henry emphasizes the completeness of Egyptian panic: "they have neither time nor heart to look back, but fear is round about them." He notes that even swift soldiers "stumble and fall toward the north" - ironically toward enemy territory rather than home.

The **"day of the Lord"** (verse 10) receives particular attention as Henry explains it as "a day of sacrifice to divine justice, to which multitudes of the sinners of Egypt shall fall as victims."

## Cross-References and Biblical Connections

Henry connects this prophecy to **2 Kings 23:29** and **24:7**, showing how Pharaoh-necho's earlier killing of King Josiah is now avenged through Babylonian victory. He also references **Proverbs 21:30-31**: "against him there is no wisdom nor counsel."

The commentary links to **Micah 4:11-12** regarding God gathering nations "as sheaves into the floor," and **Genesis 10:6** for Egypt's relationship with Ethiopia. Henry draws parallels between Old Testament judgments on Gentiles and New Testament grace: "In the Old Testament we have the word of the Lord against the Gentiles; in the New Testament we have the word of the Lord for the Gentiles."

The concluding verses about God's faithfulness to Jacob connect to broader biblical themes of divine covenant and restoration, emphasizing God's measured discipline rather than total destruction of His people.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah 47: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents the destruction of the Philistines as a manifestation of divine sovereignty over nations. The central theological theme is that **God orchestrates judgment through earthly agents**—specifically Nebuchadnezzar's Babylonian forces—to execute His will against Israel's enemies. Henry emphasizes that "Those whom God will spoil must needs be spoiled; for, if God be against them, who can be for them?"

The commentary treats this prophecy as demonstrating **God's perfect timing and omniscience**. The prophecy came "when they were in their full strength and lustre," showing that divine judgment operates independently of human probability or visible circumstances.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers:

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verses 2-3**: The "waters...out of the north" represent both military multitudes and threatening calamities. Henry notes the irony that destruction comes from the north, "whence fair weather...are said to come; but now a terrible storm comes out of that cold climate."

**Verse 6-7**: Henry's treatment of the "sword of the LORD" passage is particularly striking. He personifies the prophet's compassionate plea for peace, then provides the theological answer: "How can it be quiet, seeing the Lord hath given it a charge?" ## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects the imagery to **Revelation 17:15** (waters representing multitudes of people) and **Psalm 69:1** (waters as threatening calamities). He references **Genesis 10:14** regarding the Caphtorim's relationship to the Philistines, providing historical context for their inclusion in the judgment.

The commentary demonstrates Henry's method of using Scripture to interpret Scripture, grounding the prophetic imagery in broader biblical symbolism while maintaining focus on the immediate historical context of Babylonian conquest.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah 48: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Jeremiah 48 as God's sovereign judgment against Moab through the Chaldeans, executed about five years after Jerusalem's destruction. The central theological theme is **divine justice in action** - God as "judge over nations and kingdoms" who holds all peoples accountable, not just Israel. Henry emphasizes that this destruction is "the work of the Lord" (v. 10), demonstrating God's absolute sovereignty over nations.

A key theological insight is the **vanity of carnal confidence**. Henry notes how Moab "trusted in their works and in their treasures" (v. 7), leading to their downfall. He observes that "we forfeit the comfort of that creature which we repose that confidence in which should be reposed in God only."

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several pointed applications about **spiritual complacency**. Using Moab's description as being "at ease from his youth" and "settled on his lees" (v. 11), he warns that prosperity without trials often leads to spiritual stagnation. He notes: "While bad people are as happy as they used to be in the world it is no marvel if they are bad as they used to be."

The commentary includes a striking application of verse 10: **"Cursed be he that does the work of the Lord deceitfully."** Henry extends this beyond the immediate context to warn against half-hearted service to God, emphasizing that those who "pretend to serve God's glory, but are really serving their own ends" face divine judgment.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 7** reveals the root cause: trusting in human works and treasures rather than God. Henry explains this as why God sends enemies to "master their works and rifle their treasures."

**Verses 11-12** provide Henry's most memorable imagery - Moab as wine "settled on his lees" represents spiritual complacency. The coming "wanderers" who will "empty his vessels" symbolize how God uses trials to prevent spiritual stagnation.

**Verse 13** parallels Moab's shame in Chemosh with "Israel's shame in Beth-el," showing that false confidences ultimately disappoint all who trust in them.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to broader biblical themes. He references **Isaiah 15-16** and **Amos 2:1** for earlier Moab prophecies, and **Deuteronomy 2:9** regarding Israel's historical relationship with Moab.

Key cross-references include **Psalm 52:7** (trusting in riches rather than God), **Psalm 129:1-2** (contrasting Israel's afflictions with Moab's ease), **Psalm 55:19** (unchanged hearts due to lack of adversity), and **Malachi 1:14** (regarding negligent service to God).

The commentary concludes with hope - despite comprehensive judgment, Henry notes God's promise of **Moab's eventual restoration** (v. 48), demonstrating that divine mercy ultimately transcends judgment, reflecting the broader biblical theme of redemption even for God's enemies.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah 49 - Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents God's sovereign justice as the central theme, describing a divine "cup of trembling" that all nations must drink as judgment for their sins. He emphasizes that **"though among men might often prevails against right, yet that might shall be controlled by the Almighty, who sits in the throne, judging right."** 

The commentary demonstrates God's special concern for His covenant people, showing how divine providence operates through the principle of just retribution. Henry notes that **"the equity of divine Providence is to be acknowledged when the losses of the injured are recompensed out of the unjust gains of the injurious."** Each nation faces consequences for specific sins: the Ammonites for illegally seizing Israelite territory, the Edomites for their pride and cruelty toward Israel, and others for their various transgressions.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws several practical lessons for believers. He warns against the false security of worldly prosperity, noting that **"those who backslide and turn away from God have little reason either to take complacency or to put confidence in any worldly enjoyments whatsoever."** 

For those facing persecution or injustice, he offers comfort that God will ultimately vindicate His people: **"Though the enemies of God's Israel may make a prey of them for a while, the tables will shortly be turned."** He also provides encouragement for those leaving behind families, assuring that God's people can "in faith, commit them to him" and trust that "he will preserve them alive" when they trust in Him.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

Henry interprets the rhetorical question "Hath Israel no sons? hath he no heir?" (v.1) as God's legal challenge to Ammon's territorial claims, comparing it to a "writ of enquiry" to discover their title to Israelite lands. 

Regarding Edom's pride (v.16), he observes that their **"terribleness hath deceived thee"** - their reputation for being fearsome had made them overconfident in their own security. He notes how even Edom's renowned wisdom would fail them: **"Is wisdom no more in Teman? is counsel perished from the prudent?"**

The promise of restoration for both Ammon and Edom demonstrates that **"in human affairs: the wheel goes round"** - God's judgments are temporal, not eternal, for most nations.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this prophecy to other biblical texts. He references Jeremiah 25:15 regarding the cup of divine wrath, Amos 1:13 concerning Ammon's cruelty to pregnant women, and notes that many expressions about Edom **"are borrowed from the prophecy of Obadiah"** since **"all the prophets being inspired by one and the same Spirit, there must needs be a wonderful harmony."**

He cites Psalm 137:7 regarding Edom's joy over Israel's calamity and references the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah as a parallel to Edom's coming desolation. The commentary also draws connections to Hosea 9:1 regarding the futility of backsliders finding comfort in worldly pleasures.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah 50: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Babylon's judgment as the culmination of divine justice, noting that "Babylon was employed as the rod in God's hand for the chastising of all the other nations, and now at length that rod shall be thrown into the fire." He emphasizes God's sovereignty in using nations as instruments of judgment before ultimately judging them.

Henry draws significant typological connections, arguing that "Isaiah's prophecies of the destruction of Babylon... seem designed to typify the evangelical triumphs of all believers over the powers of darkness," while "Jeremiah's prophecies... seem designed to point at the apocalyptic triumphs of the gospel church in the latter days over the New-Testament Babylon." This prophetic duality makes Babylon both a historical enemy and a spiritual symbol.

The commentary emphasizes God's faithfulness to His covenant people despite their unfaithfulness, presenting their restoration as flowing from divine mercy rather than human merit.

## Key Practical Applications

Henry draws powerful spiritual parallels from Israel's return to Zion: "This represents the return of poor souls to God. Heaven is the Zion they aim at as their end... in all true converts there are both a sincere desire to attain the end and a constant care to keep in the way."

He stresses the importance of spiritual priorities, noting how the returning exiles "shall ask the way to Zion with their faces thitherward" - emphasizing that "they do not ask the way to heaven and set their faces towards the world."

For believers facing persecution, Henry offers encouragement: it's better to align with "the persecuted Israelites, who, though now in thraldom, are reserved for so great a glory" rather than "the persecuting Babylonians, who, though now in pomp, are reserved for so great a ruin."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

On verse 2's proclamation of Babylon's fall, Henry notes it's "spoken of as a thing done" because God's prophetic word is certain. The destruction includes even Babylon's protective idols: "Bel and Merodach... shall be confounded, and the images of them broken to pieces."

Regarding verses 4-5, Henry observes that the Israelites' tears represent "godly sorrow" - both "tears of repentance for sin" and "tears of joy for the goodness of God." Their covenant renewal must be "perpetual... never broken; and... never forgotten."

Henry poignantly describes Israel as "lost sheep" whose "own shepherds... led astray," making them vulnerable to enemies who justified their oppression by claiming Israel had "sinned against the Lord."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this passage to Psalm 9:16 regarding God being "known by those judgments which he executes." He references 1 Samuel 7:2 about Israel lamenting after the Lord, and Luke 2:48 concerning seeking the Lord with sorrow.

The commentary links to Psalm 137:1, where captives remembered Zion, and Proverbs 30:31 about the he-goat's leadership qualities. Henry also references the broader prophetic framework from Jeremiah 25:17 and 27:7.

**Core Message**: Henry presents this chapter as demonstrating God's perfect justice and mercy - destroying the oppressor while restoring His people, serving as both historical prophecy and spiritual instruction for believers' journey toward their heavenly Zion.

# Matthew Henry Commentary on Jeremiah 51 Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

Matthew Henry presents Babylon's fall as a demonstration of **divine sovereignty and justice**. Central to his interpretation is that God uses nations as instruments of His will, even pagan empires like Babylon. Henry draws several practical lessons for believers. The commentary encourages **patient trust in divine justice**. When God's people suffer injustice, they should appeal to Him as the "righteous Judge of heaven and earth" who promises: "I will plead thy cause...and take vengeance for thee, and every drop of Jerusalem's blood shall be accounted for with interest."

Henry also applies this prophetically, seeing parallels to "New-Testament Babylon" in Revelation, making the passage relevant for understanding spiritual warfare against corrupt systems.

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**Verse 7**: Henry interprets Babylon as a "golden cup" that made nations drunk both through "pleasures and debauched by her" and through "terrors and destroyed by her" - showing how worldly power corrupts through both attraction and intimidation.

**Verses 20-23**: The detailed description of Babylon as God's "battle-axe" breaking nations, armies, people, and economic systems illustrates how God uses even pagan powers to accomplish His purposes in history.

**Verse 9**: "We would have healed Babylon, but she is not healed" - Henry sees this as showing how God's people attempted to witness to their captors about idolatry's foolishness, but Babylon remained incorrigible.

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry extensively connects this passage to other biblical texts. He references **Isaiah 14:4** (golden city) and **Daniel 2:38** (head of gold) to establish Babylon's historical significance. 

Most significantly, he links this to **Revelation 17:2 and 18:3**, seeing the historical Babylon as a type of the "New-Testament Babylon" that makes "kings of the earth drunk."

He also connects to **Jeremiah 10:11** regarding Israel's mission to convince Babylonians about idol worship, showing the continuity of God's redemptive purposes even in exile.

The commentary presents Babylon's fall as both historical fulfillment and prophetic pattern, demonstrating how God's justice operates across all ages and against all forms of opposition to His kingdom.

# Matthew Henry's Commentary on Jeremiah 52: Summary

## Main Theological Themes and Interpretations

**Divine Justice and Judgment**: Henry emphasizes that this historical account serves as "the best expositor of prophecy," demonstrating God's fulfilled warnings. The destruction came because "through the anger of the LORD" Jerusalem and Judah were "cast out from his presence" due to persistent sin. Zedekiah's rebellion against Babylon was both sin and divine judgment, as "God was greatly displeased with him...and, because he was angry at Judah and Jerusalem, he put him into the hand of his own counsels" to make fatal decisions.

## Key Practical Applications

**Warning Against Spiritual Complacency**: Henry draws lessons from those who "flattered themselves" thinking they would escape captivity after seeing their brethren taken. This speaks to the danger of presuming on God's patience.

**The Futility of Human Defenses**: The siege demonstrates that "walls will not hold out long without men, any more than men without walls; nor will both together stand people in any stead without God and his protection."

**Importance of Heeding Prophetic Warning**: Henry repeatedly emphasizes that "Jeremiah had often told him what it would come to, but he would not take warning when he might have prevented it."

## Notable Verse-by-Verse Insights

**On Zedekiah's Character (vv. 1-3)**: Though not the worst king and without recorded idolatries, "he did evil in the eyes of the Lord, for he did not do the good he should have done." His rebellion against Babylon was "both his sin and his folly."

**On the Temple's Destruction (vv. 17-23)**: Henry provides detailed commentary on the pillars Jachin and Boaz, noting their symbolic meaning: "He will establish" and "In him is strength." Their destruction "signified that God would no longer establish his house nor be the strength of it."

## Cross-References to Other Scripture

Henry connects this chapter extensively to other biblical texts:
- **2 Kings 24-25**: Notes the parallel historical account
- **Psalm 51:11**: Links to David's prayer "Cast me not away from thy presence"
- **Ezekiel 17:15**: References God's displeasure with Zedekiah's treachery
- **Zechariah 8:19**: Explains the commemorative fasts instituted during captivity
- **Isaiah 64:11**: Describes the temple as "that holy and beautiful house"
- **Deuteronomy 28:15**: Warns that God's judgments will overtake the disobedient

Henry concludes that this chapter serves as both historical record and spiritual warning, demonstrating that "there is no escaping the judgments of God; they will come upon the sinner, and will overtake him, let him flee where he will."
