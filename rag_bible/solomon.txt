BOOK: Song of Solomon - Matthew Henry Commentary (Summarized)
================================================================================

Introduction
----------------------------------------
Henry begins by acknowledging that the Song of Solomon is one of the most difficult books of Scripture to understand. He notes its unique character: it contains no explicit mention of God's name, is never quoted in the New Testament, and lacks the typical markers of prophetic revelation. The Jewish rabbis cautioned young people against reading it before age thirty, lest carnal minds extract poison from what is meant to be pure and sacred.

However, Henry affirms the book's divine authority, supported by both Jewish and Christian tradition. He explains that it is an allegory—a spiritual parable using the imagery of a bridegroom and bride (portrayed as shepherd and shepherdess) to represent the mutual love between God and His people. The "letter kills" those who read only the surface, but the "spirit gives life" to those who perceive its deeper meaning.

For the Jewish church, the Song illustrated God's covenant relationship with Israel—a marriage covenant—warning against spiritual adultery (idolatry) and encouraging faithfulness while awaiting the Messiah. For the Christian church, the imagery applies even more richly: Christ is the Bridegroom, the Church is His bride. Henry points to Psalm 45, Matthew 25, Romans 7, 2 Corinthians 11, Ephesians 5, and Revelation 19-21 as New Testament keys to interpretation.

Henry concludes that while this book has shallows where children may wade and depths where elephants may swim, discovering its meaning rewards the reader with truths that come to the soul "with a more pleasing power." He urges readers to approach with reverence, soaring upward by faith and holy love into the presence of God.

Chapter 1: The Mutual Love Between Christ and His Church
----------------------------------------
Henry notes the title "Song of Songs" indicates this is the most excellent of Solomon's songs—surpassing even his other 1,005 compositions—because it contains the most of Christ. Solomon, named Jedidiah ("beloved of the Lord"), was uniquely qualified to write of divine love, just as John the beloved disciple later wrote most about love.

The chapter unfolds as a dialogue between Christ (the Bridegroom/King) and the Church (the Bride). The bride's opening cry "Let him kiss me" represents the Old Testament church longing for Christ's incarnation—no longer communication through prophets but direct communion. Henry explains that Christ's love is "better than wine" because spiritual joys surpass all sensory pleasures. His "name is as ointment poured forth"—the preaching of the gospel spreads the fragrance of Christ's knowledge everywhere.

The bride prays "Draw me, we will run after thee," illustrating the doctrine of grace: we cannot come to Christ unless drawn, yet once drawn, we must actively run. The change from singular ("draw me") to plural ("we will run") shows that one believer's zeal provokes many others.

The bride confesses being "black but comely"—black from persecution (sun-burned from trials) and from her own failures ("mine own vineyard have I not kept"), yet still beautiful in Christ's eyes. Henry applies this to the church: outwardly afflicted but inwardly glorious, sinful in herself but clothed in Christ's righteousness.

Christ responds affectionately, calling her "fairest among women" and directing her to "follow the footsteps of the flock"—to walk in the ways of godly predecessors and sit under faithful ministers. He compares her to Pharaoh's horses (strong and courageous), adorned with jewels and chains (representing graces and ordinances).

The chapter closes with mutual expressions of delight. The bride treasures Christ as "a bundle of myrrh" between her breasts—held close to the heart. Christ declares her "fair" with "doves' eyes" (humble, pure, Spirit-guided). She responds that their shared "bed is green" and their "house of cedar"—gospel ordinances are firm, fragrant, and fruitful.

Chapter 2: Christ the Rose of Sharon
----------------------------------------
Christ humbly identifies Himself as "the rose of Sharon" and "the lily of the valleys"—not a rose locked in a garden but open to all who will come. Henry emphasizes this speaks of the gospel as a "common salvation," accessible to everyone. Christ is the lily for whiteness (purity), and humble souls in the valleys see His beauty most clearly.

The church is likewise "a lily among thorns"—the wicked are thorns (worthless, hurtful, destined for burning), while believers remain innocent and pure despite being scratched and obscured by them. Henry urges: when among thorns, Christians must remain lilies, not becoming thorns themselves by rendering evil for evil.

The bride delights in Christ as "the apple tree among trees of the wood"—He alone bears fruit while other trees are merely for show. She finds both shade (protection from trials) and fruit (spiritual nourishment) in Him. His banner over her is "love"—He conquers souls not by force but by kindness. She becomes "sick of love," longing for more of Christ's presence, while His left hand supports her head and His right hand embraces her.

The famous "arise, my love" passage (vv. 10-13) depicts Christ calling the church to come away as spring returns: winter is past, flowers appear, birds sing, the turtle-dove's voice is heard. Henry applies this five ways: (1) the gospel replacing the old covenant's "winter," (2) the church delivered from persecution, (3) sinners converted to new life, (4) believers comforted after dejection, and (5) the resurrection and glory to come.

Christ calls His bride "my dove" hiding in the rock's clefts—whether praising her for sheltering in Him or gently rebuking her fearful withdrawal. He invites her prayers: "Let me hear thy voice; for sweet is thy voice."

The charge to "catch the little foxes that spoil the vines" applies both to personal mortification of sin (catching corruptions before they grow) and to opposing heresies and troublemakers in the church.

The chapter climaxes with the bride's triumphant declaration: "My beloved is mine, and I am his." She awaits the day when "shadows flee away" and prays: "Turn, my beloved"—longing for His coming like a roe leaping over mountains.

Chapter 3: Seeking and Finding Christ
----------------------------------------
The bride recounts a painful experience of spiritual desertion. She sought Christ "by night on my bed"—late, lazy seeking in darkness—and found Him not. Henry notes this represents clouded understanding and chilled affections, like the wise virgins who slumbered. She then rose to seek Him in the city streets (holy ordinances), but still found Him not. Even the watchmen (ministers) could give her no tidings.

Yet she persisted: "It was but a little that I passed from them, but I found him whom my soul loveth." Henry encourages: those who continue seeking shall find Him at last, often when nearly ready to despair. The comfort that comes after long waiting is sweeter for the delay.

When she found Him, she "held him and would not let him go"—like the women clasping Christ's feet after His resurrection. She brought Him to her "mother's house" (the church), desiring that all her relations might share communion with Him. The repeated charge to the daughters of Jerusalem—"stir not up nor awake my love"—warns against anything that might grieve the Holy Spirit and provoke Christ to withdraw.

The daughters of Jerusalem marvel: "Who is this coming out of the wilderness like pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh?" Henry applies this to Israel entering Canaan, to the church delivered from persecution, and to souls recovering from spiritual dejection—ascending heavenward with hearts lifted up like incense, perfumed with graces that are "powders of the merchant"—far-fetched and dear-bought by Christ's blood.

The bride redirects their admiration from herself to Christ, calling Him "Solomon" (meaning peace). She admires three things: (1) His bed—the church, believers' hearts, or heaven—guarded by sixty valiant men with swords, representing angels, ministers, and God's attributes protecting those who rest in Christ; (2) His chariot—the gospel or covenant of redemption—made of cedar, silver, gold, and purple, but "paved with love" at its center; (3) His crown—the honor given Him by the Father, by His persecutors (the crown of thorns), and especially by believers who crown Him King on the day of their conversion, which is also His "espousals" and "the day of the gladness of his heart."

Chapter 4: The Beauty of the Church
----------------------------------------
Christ lavishes praise on His bride: "Behold, thou art fair, my love"—repeated for emphasis. Henry clarifies this is not flattery but encouragement in dejection, teaching her what to value (the comeliness of grace He put upon her), and inviting others to join her. All her beauty derives from Him: "The spouse shines in her husband's rays."

Seven particulars describe her beauty (seven being the number of perfection): (1) Doves' eyes—clear, chaste, humble, modest, mournful, Spirit-guided, but "within thy locks," meaning partially veiled in this life; (2) Hair like a flock of goats—outward conversation, comely and agreeable to inner holiness; (3) Teeth like sheep from washing—ministers who "chew the meat" for babes in Christ, white and fruitful; (4) Lips like a scarlet thread—gracious speech washed in Christ's blood; (5) Temples like pomegranate—humility and modesty, blushing even in private; (6) Neck like David's tower—faith supplying bucklers and shields (the monuments of past saints' exploits in Hebrews 11); (7) Breasts like twin roes—the two Testaments, or two sacraments, or ministers as spiritual nurses.

Christ resolves to dwell at "the mountain of myrrh"—Mount Moriah, the temple—until day breaks and shadows flee. He pronounces her "all fair, no spot in thee"—sanctified wholly, though not yet perfect.

The bridegroom calls her "my sister, my spouse"—two relations incompatible among men but united in Christ, who shares our nature by incarnation and gives us His nature by sanctification. He invites her: "Come with me from Lebanon"—either pleasant mountains (calling her from worldly delights) or dangerous mountains with lions' dens (calling her from Satan's temptations). He exclaims: "Thou hast ravished my heart with one of thine eyes"—Christ's heart is captivated by even a single glance of faith toward Him.

Her love is "better than wine"; her ointments (graces) and garments (outward profession) are fragrant. Her lips "drop as the honeycomb"—honey and milk stored under her tongue for others' benefit.

She is a "garden enclosed, a spring shut up, a fountain sealed"—separated for God, hidden for secresy, protected for safety. The garden contains "all trees of frankincense" and "chief spices"—precious, pleasant, profitable, permanent fruits of grace.

The bride responds by acknowledging Christ as "the fountain of gardens"—without Him she would be a dry wilderness. She prays: "Awake, O north wind; come, thou south"—for the Spirit's convicting and comforting influences to make her spices flow forth. Finally she invites: "Let my beloved come into his garden and eat his pleasant fruits"—all she has is His, devoted to His glory.

Chapter 5: Spiritual Desertion and Christ's Description
----------------------------------------
Christ graciously accepts the bride's invitation: "I am come into my garden, my sister, my spouse." He gathers His own myrrh and spice, eats His honeycomb with honey, drinks wine with milk—and invites His friends: "Eat, O friends; drink, yea, drink abundantly, O beloved." Henry emphasizes the plentiful provision of the gospel: there is enough for all, enough for each; we are not straitened in Christ.

But then comes a melancholy scene. The bride confesses: "I sleep, but my heart waketh"—corruption active (spiritual drowsiness), yet grace remaining (her conscience not entirely dead). Christ knocks: "Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled: for my head is filled with dew." He pleads His suffering—His head wet with dew recalls the crown of thorns. Yet she makes frivolous excuses: "I have put off my coat; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet." Henry warns: such excuses are the language of prevailing slothfulness.

Christ "put in his hand by the hole of the door"—the work of the Spirit making her unwilling made willing. Her bowels were moved; she rose to open. Her hands dropped with myrrh upon the lock—either Christ's sweetness left there, or her own preparation to anoint Him. But alas: "My beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone." Christ's delays justly rebuke our delays.

She sought Him, called Him, but found Him not. The watchmen found her—but instead of helping, they "smote me, wounded me, took away my veil." Henry applies this to ministers who misapply the word to awakened consciences, adding affliction to the afflicted. Yet she charges the daughters of Jerusalem: "Tell him that I am sick of love."

The daughters ask: "What is thy beloved more than another beloved?" The bride responds with the famous description: "My beloved is white and ruddy, the chiefest among ten thousand"—white in spotless innocence, ruddy in bloody sufferings; white in divine glory, ruddy in human nature. Ten particulars follow: (1) head like fine gold—His sovereign dominion; (2) locks bushy and black—His eternal youth; (3) eyes like doves—His pure omniscience; (4) cheeks like beds of spices—the fragrance of His countenance; (5) lips like lilies dropping myrrh—His gracious words; (6) hands like gold rings—His powerful works; (7) bowels like bright ivory—His tender compassion; (8) legs like marble pillars—His stability; (9) countenance like Lebanon—His majesty; (10) mouth most sweet—all His words delightful.

She concludes: "He is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, and this is my friend, O daughters of Jerusalem." It is property that sweetens excellency—to see Christ as ours makes all the difference.

Chapter 6: Christ's Return and the Church's Beauty
----------------------------------------
The daughters of Jerusalem, moved by the bride's description of Christ, now ask: "Whither is thy beloved gone, that we may seek him with thee?" Henry notes that those who undervalue Christ do so because they do not know Him; when He is described, love is kindled. The bride's zeal provokes theirs.

The bride now knows where Christ is: "My beloved is gone down into his garden, to the beds of spices, to feed in the gardens, and to gather lilies." Christ's church is His garden—private, separated, planted and watered by Him. He comes to feed His flock, please Himself with His own grace in them, and gather lilies one by one (souls to Himself at death, and all together at the final harvest).

She reaffirms: "I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine"—inverting the order from chapter 2 to emphasize her own devotion first. Henry notes: every transgression in the covenant does not throw us out of covenant; rebukes and chastenings flow from covenant-love. When we lack assurance of Christ's love, we must live by faithful adherence to Him.

Christ returns and lavishes praise: "Thou art beautiful, O my love, as Tirzah, comely as Jerusalem, terrible as an army with banners." The church is beautiful like those cities—and formidable to enemies through church-discipline and the weapons of her warfare. He exclaims: "Turn away thine eyes from me, for they have overcome me"—Christ overcome by the glances of faith from His people! He repeats the same praises as in chapter 4 (hair, teeth, temples), showing His love unchanged despite her unkindness.

He prefers her above all competitors: "There are threescore queens, and fourscore concubines, and virgins without number. My dove, my undefiled is but one." The many churches and believers constitute one catholic church, all Christ's doves. Even queens and concubines blessed and praised her.

The encomium: "Who is she that looketh forth as the morning, fair as the moon, clear as the sun, and terrible as an army with banners?" Henry traces the church's light: the patriarchal church looked forth as the morning; the Jewish church was fair as the moon (borrowed, changing light); the Christian church is clear as the sun; in glory, it will shine inexpressibly bright.

Christ explains the distance and reconciliation. Even when withdrawn, He visited "the garden of nuts, to see whether the vine flourished"—watching the first buddings of grace. But "or ever I was aware, my soul made me like the chariots of Amminadib"—He could not stay away, His heart kindled to return swiftly. The prayers of His willing people were chariots that fetched Him back.

He invites: "Return, return, O Shulamite"—a fourfold call to recover lost peace. She is called Shulamite (from Solomon/Salem), denoting her relation to Christ and citizenship in heaven. "What will ye see in the Shulamite? As it were the company of two armies"—either the conflict of grace and corruption within her, or the glorious sight of the church militant and triumphant, like Jacob's vision at Mahanaim.

Chapter 7: The Church's Desire for Communion
----------------------------------------
Christ calls the church "O prince's daughter"—born from above, betrothed to royalty, with a princely disposition. He describes her beauty from foot to head (as Isaiah 1:6 reckons Israel's corruption from sole to head), giving more honor to less honorable parts. Ten particulars again, matching the bride's ten descriptions of Him in chapter 5: (1) feet beautiful with shoes—standing fast in gospel liberty; (2) joints of thighs like jewels—the church's unity and steadiness; (3) navel like a goblet—spiritual health; (4) belly like a heap of wheat—nourishment for the body; (5) breasts like twin roes—consolation and love; (6) neck like ivory tower—faith, strong and precious; (7) eyes like fishpools—clear understanding, tears for sin; (8) nose like Lebanon's tower—courage facing Damascus (enemies); (9) head like Carmel—lifted above storms, pointing heavenward; (10) hair like purple—royal dignity throughout.

Christ's complacency: "The king is held in the galleries"—He delights to dwell with His people. "How fair and how pleasant art thou, O love, for delights!" He resolves to take hold of her as a palm tree (which flourishes under weight) and enjoy her fruits. Her breasts like clusters of grapes, her breath like apples, her mouth "like the best wine"—going down sweetly, causing sleeping lips to speak. The spouse interjects: "for my beloved"—all is from Him and for Him.

The bride responds: "I am my beloved's, and his desire is toward me"—what comfort that Christ desires His people! She invites: "Come, my beloved, let us go forth into the field; let us lodge in the villages." Henry notes: those who would commune with Christ must go forth from the world's distractions. Solitude befriends communion. She will rise early to the vineyards—improving every opportunity. She will lodge in humble cottages if He is there—His presence converts them to palaces.

She wants to examine her own soul with Christ: "Let us see if the vine flourish, whether the tender grape appear." Our souls are our vineyards; we must inspect them, especially watching young and tender graces lest they be nipped or blasted. Christ's presence makes the vine flourish.

She promises her best: "There will I give thee my loves." At her gates are "all manner of pleasant fruits, new and old, which I have laid up for thee, O my beloved." Henry: we must have fruits for all occasions—not only new experience but old treasures, nor only old but continually adding new. Those who truly love Christ think all they have too little for Him—it is all from Him, and therefore should be all for Him.

Chapter 8: The Conclusion—Love Strong as Death
----------------------------------------
The bride wishes: "O that thou wert as my brother, that sucked the breasts of my mother!" Henry applies this to the Old Testament church longing for Christ's incarnation, and to all believers desiring closer communion. If Christ were her brother, she could kiss Him publicly without reproach. Since His incarnation, the church can more openly own Him—like David dancing before the ark. She would bring Him into her mother's house (the church), where He would instruct her, and she would cause Him to drink her spiced wine—the exercise of grace, acceptable to Christ.

She again expects His support: "His left hand should be under my head, and his right hand should embrace me"—as He laid His hand on John in Revelation 1:17. She charges the daughters of Jerusalem: "Why should you stir up my love?"—reasoning with them against anything that would provoke Christ to withdraw.

The daughters admire: "Who is this that cometh up from the wilderness, leaning upon her beloved?" Henry applies this to conversion (coming out of a barren wilderness by Christ's strength), to consolation (a humbled soul finding peace), and to salvation (living and dying by faith in Christ). The bride recounts past experiences: "I raised thee up under the apple tree"—prevailing with Christ in prayer. "There thy mother brought thee forth"—believers travailing in convictions until comfort is born.

She prays: "Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm"—to be engraven on His breast-plate like Israel's tribes on Aaron's, never to be removed. She pleads the power of love: "Love is strong as death"—Christ's love broke through death; our love makes us dead to all else. "Jealousy is cruel as the grave"—holy jealousy guards against anything that would draw us from Christ. "Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it"—neither terrors nor temptations can overcome true love. "If a man would give all the substance of his house for love, it would utterly be contemned"—Christ seeks not our wealth but our hearts.

The bride intercedes for her "little sister" who has "no breasts"—whether the Gentile world or uncalled elect souls, not yet grown to maturity. Christ answers: "If she be a wall, we will build upon her a palace of silver"—if the good work begins, it will be completed. The bride testifies: "I am a wall, and my breasts like towers: then was I in his eyes as one that found favour"—evidence that Christ will do the same for her little sister.

Concerning the vineyard: Solomon let out his vineyard at Baal-hamon to keepers who each owed 1000 pieces of silver. The bride resolves: "My vineyard, which is mine, is before me"—she had formerly neglected it (chapter 1:6), but now will watch carefully. Christ must have His due (1000), and the keepers their wages (200)—those who work for Christ work for themselves.

The song concludes with parting requests. Christ asks: "Thou that dwellest in the gardens, the companions hearken to thy voice: cause me to hear it"—He courts our prayers, desires our communion, and loves to hear from us. The bride responds: "Make haste, my beloved, and be thou like to a roe or a young hart upon the mountains of spices"—longing for His return. Henry concludes: those who truly love Christ long for His second coming; our sweetest communion here should make us breathe the more earnestly after the immediate vision of Him in glory. "Even so, come, Lord Jesus, come quickly."
